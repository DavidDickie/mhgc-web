## MEGA HYPER GALACTIC CONQUEST

MHGC is your typical expand / grow / kill everyone else space conquest game. The name is derived from an old game I wrote and ran at Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute (RPI or the 'tute) and HyperEconomic Diplomacy (a.k.a. GeoDiplomacy or Geodip). I was playing Geodip at the time I became motivated enough to ruin my life with this incredible time sink. The game is at best vaguely related to either of these preceding games, so don't base any strategies on the name selection.

In the game, players attempt to balance expansion, capital investment, technology investment, and military conquest in order to be crowned "Grand Poohba of the Universe."

MHGC was originally written to play by Email. Now it runs in your web browser: you give your orders on screen, and your turn results come back on screen. "PLAYING IN YOUR BROWSER" explains the screen; everything after it is the game.

## JOINING A GAME

MHGC is FREE, FREE, FREE.

To join a game, open the game list in your browser (or a link to the game), pick the game, and take an open seat with a race name and a password of your own; or start a new game and invite your friends. Your password lets you play your race from any browser or device. Seats that no human takes are filled by NPRs. See "THE GAME LIST" and "PASSWORDS AND LOGGING IN" below for the details.

## PLAYING IN YOUR BROWSER

This chapter is about the screen: where things are and how to give your orders. The rest of the rulebook is the game itself. Press **?** at any time (or the **?** button at the top right) to open these rules; the search box finds any word, and Enter jumps to the next match.

### THE GAME LIST

The first screen is the list of games. Each game shows its turn, its size, when it last changed, and a chip for every human seat: open, waiting, submitted (✓), or "computer" for a race the host handed to the computer. The host is marked ★ ("★ host only" when the host plays no race) and the races you are logged in to are highlighted. Each game also says whose orders the turn is waiting for.

- **New game** creates a game. You choose a game name, your own name, the size (4, 8, 9, 12, 16, 20 or 25 empires; see "THE GAMEBOARD"), how many seats are for humans, and how much the NPRs chatter. Then either **I'll play a race**: your race name and its password (twice), and you take seat 1 and are the host; or **Host only, no race**: you run the game without playing in it (see "THE HOST"). Either way the game needs a **host password**: when you play a race, "Use my race password as the host password" (ticked at first) makes it the same as your race password; untick it to choose another (twice). You can use the same host password for all the games you host, so it is easy to remember; the browser can remember the last one you typed for your next New game (only in this browser).
- **Open / join** (or a click on the game's name) opens the game. It offers what you can do there:
  - **Play an existing race:** pick a race from the game's human races and enter its password.
  - **Take an open seat**, if there is one: pick the seat, give your name and choose a password (twice). Before the first turn you also choose the race name; from turn 1 on, a newcomer takes over the race that sits in that seat, with its name, and the dialog shows it.
  - **I'm the host:** the host password logs you in as the host.
- **Play** opens a game you are logged in to in this browser (**Play the <race>** when you hold several races there; **Host view** for a host login).
- **Leave** gives up your seat so someone else can take it. Your empire stays in the game; its password is cleared and every browser logged in to it is logged out. (With several races in a game, use "Leave this seat" in the game screen's ☰ menu.) If the host leaves and has a host password, they stay host through the host login; otherwise the next seated player becomes host.
- **Drop** deletes a game. Any seated player can do it, the host, or anyone once nobody is seated.

The ⌂ button at the top left of the game screen takes you back to the game list.

### PASSWORDS AND LOGGING IN

Every human race has a password, chosen by whoever takes the seat; it needs at least 4 characters. Logging in from a browser keeps you logged in there (the browser stores a login key for that race, never the password), so you only type the password again on a new browser or device, or after logging out. You can be logged in to the same race from any number of browsers at once.

- **Wrong passwords:** "Wrong password for the <race>". Try again as often as you like: there is no limit and no waiting.
- **Change password** (in the ☰ menu) needs your current password. Every other browser logged in to the race is logged out; this one stays in.
- **Log out of this race** (☰ menu) logs this browser out; **Log out everywhere** logs out every browser logged in to the race.
- **Forgotten your password?** Ask the host: the host can give your race a temporary password. Every browser logged in to it is logged out, and when you log in with the temporary password you must choose a new one before you can play.
- **Games from before passwords:** a browser that already held a seat keeps it, but on its next visit it must choose a password for that race before it can play on. A race nobody has set a password for can't be logged in to, and nobody can take it as a seat; if its old browser is lost, the host resets its password and tells the player the temporary one.

Passwords travel encrypted (https) on the hosted game; the server keeps only a scrambled form of each one, which can't be turned back into the password.

### PLAYING SEVERAL RACES

One person may play more than one race in a game: take another open seat, or log in to another race, from **Log in to another race or take a seat…** in the ☰ menu (or from the game list). Each race is a separate player in every way: its own view, orders, submission, chat and statistics. When this browser holds several races of a game, the race switcher in the top bar changes between them; each switch loads that race's screen afresh, so nothing of one race's view is ever shown with another. The turn waits for every human race, so submit each of your races. Holding several races changes nothing in the rules (see "THE HOST" for the export).

### THE GAME SCREEN

Across the top are the game and your race, the turn picker (◀ ▶, for history), your EU stockpile, IU supply depot, victory points, systems, population, ships and technologies, whether your orders are submitted and whom the turn is waiting for, and the **Check orders**, **Submit turn** / **Withdraw** and (for the host) **Force turn** buttons. Then the race switcher (when you hold several races here) and the **☰** menu: change password, log out, leave the seat, log in to another race or take a seat, and for the host the host controls and the export. The galaxy map fills the left; the panels (tabs) are on the right.

### THE MAP AND ITS LAYERS

The map is the galaxy as you know it. It wraps in both directions, like the board, and it is drawn from your home system: your home is always at the centre (50,50 on the 100 board, 62,62 on the 125 board, 37,37 on the 75 board, 25,25 on the 50 board), with y increasing upwards. Drag to pan, scroll (or + and −) to zoom, ⌂ to centre on home. The small overview map at the bottom right shows the whole board; click it to jump there. "Go to system…" finds any system you know by name.

- **Systems** are coloured by their owner's relation to you: yours, allied, neutral, at war, not yet contacted, rebels, or unowned. A system whose habitability you know is drawn larger the more habitable it is (an unknown one is a small diamond); an orange square pip marks Metal Rich and a green round pip Agriculture Rich. Your own systems carry a green population badge when zoomed in, notches for their starport, and a gold ring on your home. A dashed red ring means under attack or interdicted.
- **Ships** not in a fleet are chevrons with a count: yours point up and right of the square, other races' down and left. Scanned foreign ships show a faint trail from where they moved from.
- **Fleets** are hexagons with the number of ships in them: yours to the right of the square, with the fleet's name when zoomed in; a fleet your draft is forming this turn is dashed and labelled "(forming)". Other races' fleets that your scans show are hexagons to the left of the square, in their colour. Click a fleet's hexagon to select that fleet. Zoomed out, a square shows one hexagon of each kind, with "+n" for more fleets.
- **The selected fleet or ship** has a pink ring around it (pulsing; a steady thick ring if your system is set to reduce motion). If it is off the map, or under the selection panel, a pink arrow on the edge of the map points to it and says how far away it is; click the arrow to bring it into view.
- **Battles** of this turn are starbursts.
- **Movement:** standing orders are faint blue paths, this turn's draft moves dashed yellow, with a dot per turn and the number of turns to arrive; the selected unit's course is bright.
- **Scans:** squares inside your scans now are tinted blue; squares you have scanned before but cannot see now are grey; squares you have never scanned are dark, outlined where your explored space ends. A system can hide in the dark.
- **Map memory:** systems you have seen but are not scanning now are dimmed and dashed, with the latest name, owner, habitability and MR/AR you know of (the tooltip and the selection panel say as of which turn).

The **Layers** menu turns each of these on or off (grid and coordinates, system names, scan coverage, scan history, map memory, movement orders, foreign ships, battles) and holds the legend. Hovering over a square shows what is there.

With the map focused, the arrow keys move a cursor (Ctrl for 10 squares, Shift to pan), Enter selects the square, M moves the selected fleet or ship to the cursor, H centres on home, + and − zoom and Esc cancels. Elsewhere, ? opens the rules and / jumps to "Go to system".

### THE SELECTION PANEL

Click a square to open the selection panel over the map. It shows everything there that you can see, with buttons for the orders that make sense:

- **Your system:** its population, mood, habitability, status, starport, infrastructure, stockpiles, profiles, production, scan range, governor, artifacts and trade routes, and any of your draft orders that mention it. The buttons give the common system orders (Build ship, Build troops, Profile, Infrastructure, Starport +1, Artifact, AU release, IU to depot, and Fleet from ships here); the "Other orders" picker lists every other system order, and "Troop orders" those for your troops on the system.
- **Your ships here that are not in a fleet** are listed, each with a tick box, and **Select all**. Tick some and press **Make fleet** to make a fleet of them (see "Making a fleet" below), or pick one of your fleets on the square and press **Add to fleet** (an AF order).
- **Your fleet or ship:** its members or cargo, speed, posture, role, attack and defence. Every fleet on the square has its own card; click its name to select it. **Move (click map)** first brings the unit into view if it is off the map, then a pink line follows the pointer from the unit's ring while you choose; a click on the destination gives the move order (the dashed route shows the turns it takes at the fleet's speed, which is its slowest ship's unless you set one); there are also Move to system, Intercept (click the target), Guard, Speed, Posture, Role, Load (one form for population, IUs, AUs and combat troops at once, showing the cargo room used and left; it starts from any loads of that ship or fleet already in your draft and replaces them; it counts the unloads already in your draft, so a full transport can unload and then load something else on the same turn, and puts the new loads after those unloads), Unload (the same for unloading and landing troops: at an unowned empty system the population lands first, which claims it, then the IUs, AUs and troops; at another race's system the population, IUs and AUs are a gift; it counts what earlier loads in your draft put aboard), the other troop orders, Join fleet, Add and Remove ships, Merge into… (for a fleet), and Disband, plus the "Other orders" picker for the rest. A transport carrying population at an unowned empty system offers "Claim <system> (land population)", which adds the unloads in the right sequence.
- **Other races' systems and ships:** what your scans show. With one of your units selected, a foreign ship has an Intercept button.
- **Battles** on the square, shown in full.

A selected fleet or ship stays selected when you click elsewhere, so the panel offers "Send … here"; right-clicking a square (or M) moves it there at once. A move made on the map replaces that unit's earlier move order rather than adding another.

**Making a fleet.** Make fleet asks for the fleet's name, with a free name filled in (the system's name and a number). A name is up to 12 letters, digits, _ - or . with no spaces, and must differ from your other fleets. Other races may have fleets with the same name; an order always refers to your own fleet. Make fleet adds a Create Fleet order and Add to Fleet orders for the ticked ships (CF, then AF, up to 10 ships a line) to the end of your draft; if any of those ships already had their own move orders in the draft, the dialog offers to drop them, since a ship in a fleet moves with the fleet. Orders are carried out in the order of your draft and before movement, and CF and AF act at once, so the new fleet appears on the map as forming and **can be given its move order this turn**: select it, Move (click map), and it sets off when the turn runs. A move order for a forming fleet is always placed after the CF that creates it. "Cancel this fleet" on a forming fleet's card takes its CF and every order naming it out of the draft. Fleet from ships here (CFXY) is different: its ships join only at the end of movement, so that fleet can't move this turn; a move order given to it is carried out from the next turn, and the panel and the move message say so. CFXY is the way to gather ships that are being built this turn.

**Merging fleets.** A selected fleet's card has **Merge into…**, which offers your other fleets on the same square (it is greyed out when there are none; fleets merge only at the same square). Pick one and press Merge: a Merge Fleets order (MGF) goes to the end of your draft and the fleet it merges into is selected. MGF acts at once, like Add to Fleet, so straight away the map shows one hexagon with all the ships, the Forces tab lists the merged fleet only, and the merged fleet moves this turn with its own orders (a move order you gave the absorbed fleet no longer applies, and Check orders says so). A later order in the draft that still names the absorbed fleet will fail; Check orders warns about it and offers a Fix that names the merged fleet instead. The absorbed fleet's name is free at once, so a Create Fleet later in the same draft can use it again.

### GIVING ORDERS

Every order is an action in a form. The buttons on the selection panel and on the tabs open the form for that order with what you clicked already filled in; the order picker at the top of the Orders tab ("New order…") lists every order by category. The forms pick from what you actually have: your ships, fleets, troops, spies and leaders; your systems, or any known system by name; the races you have contacted; the technologies you can buy (or own, to gift); the ship, troop and artifact types with their costs. Coordinates have a **Pick on map** button. Each form shows the order's classic code and its classic text as you fill it in, and **More from the rulebook** opens that order's entry in these rules. Adding the order puts it into your draft; nothing is final until the turn runs.

The **Orders** tab is your draft: the queue of orders in the sequence they will run (see "SEQUENCING TRICKS" for why the sequence matters). Each order shows its classic text and a description. ↑ and ↓ (or dragging) reorder it, ✎ reopens its form, ✕ deletes it, and "Clear all" empties the draft.

### THE CLASSIC ORDER TEXT

Under the draft, **Classic order text** holds your whole draft as classic order lines (see "ORDER FORMATS"). Veterans can type or paste orders there, one per line, and press **Use this text as the draft**; any line that doesn't parse is reported with its line number, and nothing changes until every line is right. **Reset to draft** throws away what you typed. Orders given through the forms and typed orders are the same thing: each form shows the line it adds, and each typed line becomes an order in the draft.

### CHECK ORDERS, SUBMIT AND WITHDRAW

**Check orders** runs your draft against the current state of the game and marks each order ✓, or ✗ with the reason (not enough EUs, you don't own that system, and so on). It is a check, not a turn preview: nothing moves, no dice are rolled, and other players' orders aren't known.

Some orders are valid but probably don't do what you mean. These get a **⚠ warning** in amber under the order, and the Check orders button shows how many there are (the draft list shows them as soon as you change the draft, without pressing Check). Warnings never stop you submitting:

- **Loading or unloading after a warp.** All orders run before movement, and a move order (MO, MS, MF, MFS, I, FIS, G) only sets where the unit is going, so a load, unload, troop load or landing in the same turn as a move happens where the unit is now and works; putting it after the move in the list makes no difference, and you get no warning for it. A warp tunnel jump (WTP, WTPF) is the exception: it moves the unit at once, so a cargo order after it acts at the far end. If it works there you get a warning such as "Ship 9835 loads at Hamburgia (after the warp in order 4), not at Goosebeak.", with a **Fix** that moves the load before the warp (offered when that works). If it fails there, the ✗ error says why ("Ship 9835 was moved to Hamburgia by your warp order (order 4) before this load, so it tries to load at Hamburgia: ... Put the load before the warp order."), with the same Fix.
- **Unloading cargo where you loaded it this turn, then moving.** A load and an unload of the same cargo by the same unit at the same place, for a unit that moves this turn, puts the cargo straight back ashore before the unit leaves: "Ship 9835 unloads at Goosebeak, where order 1 loaded it, before it moves to Hamburgia (orders run before movement), so the cargo stays at Goosebeak. To unload at Hamburgia, give the unload order next turn." Drop pods (DOCT) drop after movement, at the destination, so they get no warning.

A load or unload that fails because the unit isn't there yet says where it is and when it arrives, from its move orders: "Ship 9835 is in deep space at 53,50, still on its way to Goosebeak (arrives in 2 turns, at the end of turn 8). It can load once it's there: give the load order on turn 9." Only names you know are used (your systems, the names on your map), otherwise coordinates.
- **Names that a rename changes.** Orders run in the order listed and a rename (NSY for a system, CLN for a leader, CRN for your race) takes effect at once. An order after the rename that still uses the old name, or one before it that already uses the new name, fails: "MS 9835 Goosebeak: Goosebeak is renamed to Hamburgia by order 3, so this order should use Hamburgia." Press **Fix** to rewrite the order with the right name. Two renames to the same name, or a second rename of a leader or of your race, are warned about too.
- **A build count that can't all be built** (see Build a ship).

**Submit turn** hands in your draft. Until the turn runs you can keep changing your orders: any change withdraws the submission (you are told so), and **Withdraw** does it on purpose. Submit again when you are ready; the orders that run are the ones you last submitted.

### WHEN THE TURN RUNS

The turn runs as soon as every human seat has submitted (an open seat holds the turn up until someone takes it), or when the host presses **Force turn**. Before forcing, the host is told who hasn't submitted. What happens to them depends on their AOMT setting (below; on by default): with it on, the draft they were working on is run as if they had submitted it, or, if it is empty, the computer writes their orders; with it off, their empires give no orders that turn and the draft is ignored. Everyone sees "(forced by the host)" in the chat. A host login that plays no race is never waited for.

**Autogenerate orders on missed turn (AOMT).** So that you don't lose a turn when the host forces one before you have submitted, **Autogenerate orders on missed turn (AOMT)** is on for every player unless you switch it off on the Empire tab (or give the order `AOMT OFF`; see "Autogenerate orders on missed turn" in the empire orders). A change takes effect at once and stays until you change it again; when you leave the seat it goes back to on for the next player. With it on, a turn forced before you submitted still runs: **if you have a draft, it runs as if you had submitted it**, and your report for that turn starts with "You missed turn N; AOMT is on, so your unsubmitted draft was run as if you had submitted it". **If your draft is empty** (or holds nothing but the AOMT setting), the computer writes your orders from exactly what you can see (your own map, report and empire; nothing hidden from you), as a careful caretaker. It researches, builds, settles, defends and carries on what is under way, but it says nothing and changes nothing between you and other races: no messages, no declarations of war or alliance, no peace offers, no gifts, no trade terms or technology trades, no renamed systems or race, no resignation. Your report for that turn then starts with "You missed turn N; your orders were generated automatically (AOMT is on)", and the orders it gave are listed in your order results, each with a short note of why. Your race stays yours: nobody else is told, and it is still a human race to everyone. A turn you did submit always runs your own orders.

In a game where no human race gives orders (only computer empires, or the last human race was handed to the computer) turns never run by themselves: everyone sees "Waiting for the host to run turn N", and the host presses **Run turn** (no confirmation, since nobody is waiting) or **Run 5 turns**, which runs them one after another, saving each and refreshing the screen after each, and stops when the game ends.

You don't need to reload: the screen notices when the turn has run, tells you so, and opens the Report tab with the new results. A new turn starts with an empty draft; standing orders such as a ship's destination carry on by themselves.

### THE REPORT TAB

The Report tab is your turn results (see "TURN RESULTS" for what each part means). At the top, tiles show your EU, IU depot, VP, systems, population, NNP, production and warships, with the change from the turn before, and a banner if there were battles. Below are sections you can open and close: messages, order results (✓ or ✗ with the reason), events grouped by kind with links to the system on the map, battles (the sides, each phase and the outcome, with "Show on map"), the maintenance ledger, the foreign systems and ships in your scans (sortable; a row takes you to the map) and your statistics and ranks.

**Report: Standard | Classic text.** The picker at the top of the Report tab switches to the classic text report, the turn results laid out as the 2008 game mailed them: the header, order problems, messages, rebellion, the maintenance listing with a block per system (initial stocks, profile, production, AU eaten, artifact upkeep, totals), combat drops, combat, random events, the staff reports (economic, victory points, statistics, relations), leaders in transit, intelligence (your spies), your ships, fleets and systems, and what you scanned, in plain monospace text. It is the same results as the standard report, for your empire only, so it shows nothing more; it works for earlier turns too. **Copy** puts it on the clipboard and **Download (.txt)** saves it as a file. The browser remembers which style you chose.

### THE OTHER TABS

- **Empire:** your economy at a glance, the autosupply switch (AUTOSISD), Rename race, the **Autogenerate orders on missed turn (AOMT)** checkbox (see "WHEN THE TURN RUNS"; it switches at once, with no order needed), a sortable and filterable table of all your systems (a row takes you to the system), your artifacts and your trade routes.
- **Production:** one row per system, largest population first (click a heading to sort by another column; the browser remembers your choice), with its production profile as three boxes (AU, IU and EU, each 10 to 80) and an estimate of the turn ahead: mood after feeding, the AU and IU stockpiles at the end of the turn, EU produced, and flags for infrastructure below population (⚠IF), technologies the system could install now (★) and uprising risk. Each box is edited on its own; a row whose three values don't total exactly 100 turns red with "must total 100 (now N)" and its estimate is dimmed until you fix it. Below are the empire's EU and IU depot after the turn and the projected maintenance listing. **Generate CSP orders** turns your changed rows into system profile orders (CSP) in your draft, skipping any row that doesn't total 100 (it tells you how many). ↺ resets a row and "Reset all" every row. Each row's **Preset…** list sets the three boxes at once, and **Set all to…** (next to "Reset all") sets every row; a preset counts as an edit like typing, so the estimate updates and Generate CSP orders writes it. The presets (AU / IU / EU %) are: Very early 50/40/10, Early 20/60/20, Balanced 20/40/40, Late 10/10/80 and War 10/80/10. Each system's output is rounded down to whole AU, IU and EU, so a split can waste a fraction of a unit in each. The **±** (Tweak) button on a row moves the three shares by up to 3 points each to the split that produces the most units in total without lowering any of AU, IU or EU (so feeding and happiness are never worse); if no such split beats yours, the row says "Already optimal". For example, a system producing 42.2 units makes 9 AU at 23% (9.7, rounded down) but 10 AU at 24%. The note under the split says what was gained ("+2 units: AU 19→21"), and the change counts as an edit like typing. **± Tweak all** (between "Set all to…" and "Reset all") tweaks every row with a valid split. The tweak helps small and middling systems: once a system's production before the split is 100 or more, every point is worth at least a whole unit of whatever it is moved from, so nothing can be gained. The estimate uses the real rules on your own empire only, with your draft orders; it leaves out random events, combat, uprisings, emperor days, trade income and anything another empire does.
- **Tech:** the technology tree by cost, each technology owned, buyable or locked, with its prerequisites, what it unlocks and its VP. **Buy** gives the purchase order (PT) and **Crash** the crash research order (CRP).
- **Forces:** tables of your fleets, ships and troops, sortable. Ship types are grouped as in the Build ship picker (see "Build a ship"). A ship in a fleet is listed under its fleet: each fleet row shows how many ships it has and what they are ("2 Transport · 1 Frigate"), and ▸ opens the fleet's ships, with the same columns as the Ships table. The **Ships** table lists the ships not in a fleet ("Ships (5 loose)"); tick **Include ships in fleets** to list every ship. Both count the fleets your draft is forming and the ships it adds to or takes out of fleets. Ships can be filtered by text, type or "idle only" (no orders, not in a fleet); ships with draft orders are marked ✎. Rows take you to the unit. Your sorts, filters and open fleets are remembered by this browser.
- **Leaders:** each leader with his or her five ratings, post, age and biography, with Move to system, Move to fleet, Rename and Put on ice; each spy with its post, Move and a mission picker.
- **Races:** the races you have contacted, human or computer, with your stance towards them (you never see theirs towards you) and buttons for Ally, Declare war, Offer peace or End alliance, Gift EU, Gift tech, Gift ship, Trade tech (computer races), Deny and Split trade, Send system info, Message, and Chat privately (human races).
- **Stats:** your statistics over every turn as small charts, with your value and rank on each turn, or as a table.
- **Chat:** game-wide chat, readable by everyone, and a private thread with each human race you have contacted (see "ALLIANCES" for contact). Chat is between turns and immediate; MESSAGE orders are still delivered with the turn results. NPRs don't read chat, but they boast in the game-wide thread; the host sets how chatty they are here. An NPR may also offer you a technology trade in a private thread ("The GROACHI offer Core Mining for your Bioengineered Foods. Accept by this turn's end."): each **Accept** button puts the matching TTNPR order in your orders (only once; picking another alternative of the same offer replaces it; an offer "for any 1000 EU technology of yours" has one button, which adds `TTNPR ANY 1000 ...`), and the trade happens when the turn runs. The offer is good for the orders of the turn it names; after that turn it shows as expired. Only the race it was made to sees it, and only on the current turn.
- **Rules:** these rules, with a contents list and search, kept where you left off when you switch tabs. The **?** button opens the same rules in a larger window.

### WHEN THE GAME ENDS

When someone wins, everyone in the game gets **Final standings**: a table of every race, human and computer, and charts of victory points, systems, population, production, technologies, stockpiles, warships, troops and happiness over every turn, one line per race (humans solid, computer empires dashed), coloured as on your map or by race. Until then nobody sees another race's figures. Finished games also show Final standings in the game list. **Export game for analysis** downloads the whole game (every turn's orders and results, every race's figures, the computer empires' reasoning and the host audit log) as a zip: any player may do this once the game is over, and the host may do it at any time, in any game (☰ menu, **Export for analysis**). An export shows the host everything, other humans' races included; that is accepted, and it is one more reason to pick a host you trust. Holding several races doesn't change who may export.

### THE HOST

The host runs the game. The creator is the host, playing seat 1 or hosting without a race. The host's powers are in the top bar and in **Host controls…** in the ☰ menu, and everyone sees a notice in the game-wide chat when the host changes the game:

- **Force turn**, or **Run turn** in a game no human gives orders in (see "WHEN THE TURN RUNS").
- **Reset password** for another race (see "PASSWORDS AND LOGGING IN"). The notice names the race.
- **Hand to the computer:** a human race becomes a computer empire for good, played by the bot from its own view (its own last report; nothing else it couldn't have seen). Its password, logins, draft and submission are dropped, and the turn no longer waits for it. Its relations, holdings, name, victory points and ranking carry over: every race is ranked and can win, so only who gives its orders changes. The host can't hand over the host's own race. An open human seat can be handed over too, so the game no longer waits for it.
- **Open for a human:** a computer empire becomes an open human seat with its name and empire as they stand; its bot memory and personality are dropped. A new player takes it like any open seat, with a password; its victory points and ranking carry on unchanged. Until someone takes it, it gives no orders and, as any open seat, holds up the automatic turn: the host may want to force turns meanwhile. A race handed to the computer can be opened again the same way.
- **Export for analysis** at any time (see "WHEN THE GAME ENDS").
- **Computer chatter** (Chat tab).
- **Host password:** every game has one, chosen when it is created. **Change the host password…** in the ☰ menu. The host password opens the **host login** from the game dialog ("I'm the host"), from any browser. A game from before host passwords were required asks its host to set one on the next visit, before the game opens.

**The host handicap.** If the host plays a race in a game with at least one other human player, the host's race can't win and earns no ranking VP: it gets no victory points for leading in the statistics, and it is left out of the rankings altogether, so its figures push nobody down and the other races are ranked as if it weren't there. Reaching the win target doesn't end the game for it. The host's race keeps its technology and system VP, and sees its own statistics, marked "(host, unranked)"; the Report and Empire tabs say "As host of a multi-player game your race can't win and earns no ranking VP". It counts only human players: open seats, computer empires and races handed to the computer don't, so a host playing alone against the computer, or hosting without a race, is not handicapped. It is worked out again every turn, so it starts the turn after a second player joins and stops the turn after the last other player leaves or is handed to the computer. Final standings list a handicapped host's race after the others, without a rank.

The **host login** holds no seat and is never waited for. Besides the powers above it can **View as** any race, human or computer: the "View as" picker in the top bar shows exactly what that race sees (its map, report, draft and submitted orders, statistics and chat), under a banner "Viewing as the <race> (host)". Viewing is read-only: the host can't give orders, submit or chat for another race. If the host also plays a race, that race is separate: the host's own race plays as usual, and View as needs the host login. Other players get no notice of View as, but every View as and every other host action is written to the host audit log, which the export includes.


### LOOKING AT EARLIER TURNS

The turn picker at the top (◀, the list, ▶) shows any earlier turn: the map, the selection panel and the report as they were then. A banner says which turn you are looking at, with "Back to the current turn". Orders can't be given while you look at history.

## CONVENTIONS

To make it easier to correlate sections of the rules with the specific orders used to implement your strategy, if orders are related to or referenced by a section, the order name is inserted in parenthesis () in the section text. Occasionally order options, or key words that are used to indicate a particular condition on turn results, will be discussed as well; these are inserted into the text enclosed in square brackets []. For instance, you might see something like:

"When ordering Combat Troops to change their posture (CCP) to Attack [A] or Defend [D] you should..."

The order names are the classic order codes. In the web game every order is an action you pick from a form (the form shows its code), and every order can still be typed in the classic format (see "GIVING ORDERS" and "ORDERS").

## TURN PROCESSING

Each player gives his or her orders in the browser and submits them (Submit turn). The turn runs as soon as every human player has submitted orders, or when the host forces the turn (see "THE HOST" for the host's powers). There are no deadlines, and your empire is never handed over to the computer automatically (only the host can do that, and everyone is told): if the host forces a turn before you have submitted, your empire simply gives no orders that turn (it still produces, grows and pays maintenance as usual).

Until the turn runs you may change your orders and submit again; the last orders you submitted are the ones used. Everyone's screen updates by itself when the turn has run.

If you want out, you may resign (RESIGN). Your empire becomes an NPR, and like any dropped player it is "buffed" up to make it harder for your neighbors to pick it apart (see "NPRs").

## TURN ORDER

(1) In sequence provided in player orders:

- Give orders to ships and Combat Troops (change destinations, postures, etc.) \*
- Load/Unload cargo and troops, create trade route \*
- Move IUs to Supply Depot
- Build spacecraft / troops / infrastructure / starport / etc.
- Buy technology
- Gift EUs, Spy, Alliances and / or War declared between empires
- Move leaders

(2) Produce

(3) Feed pop, Happiness check and results (including rebellion)

(4) Grow pop

(5) Pay maintenance (demobilize troops/spacecraft if necessary)

(6) Move followed by Intercept followed by Guard \*

(7) Drop Combat Troops

(8) Combat (including change of status if one happens, i.e. system becomes interdicted), ship to ship, then ship to ground, then ground to ground

(9) Spy activities

(10) Random events

(11) Scans

\* Except for newly built ships

In step (1), the empires' orders are processed in a random order each turn; each empire's orders are processed in the sequence given. So if another empire gifts you EUs or a technology, there is a 50/50 chance it arrives before your orders run (your turn results tell you which happened).

## VICTORY CONDITIONS

The first player to reach 100 victory points wins. (Games started before 22 November 2026 keep the target they were started with, 90; the VP figure on the top bar shows your game's target.) Victory is checked at the end of every turn. If several players reach the target or more on the same turn, they are tied, and the tie is decided by the leader in NNP. You get victory points for each technology over 250 EUs you have and two for each system you have (an empty system still held by your troops counts). Future Technology, unlike regular technologies, does not provide an associated new capability or artifact and can be purchased more than once. Each future technology you purchase is worth four additional victory points. While these conditions do not guarantee conquering the galaxy, it is an attempt to select a reasonable point to end the game such that things do not drag out after many of the players have been eliminated. A game can end earlier at the request of the majority of the players; under these conditions, the winner will be decided on by a vote of all players, one vote per system owned, majority wins. Victory points for technology are scored per the following table:

| Technology cost | VP |
|---|---|
| 100, 250 EU technologies | 0 |
| 500, 1000 EU technologies | 1 |
| 2000, 3000 EU technologies | 2 |
| 4000 EU technologies | 3 |
| FUTURE TECH (each) | 4 |

You also receive VP for leading in certain statistics, 2 points for 1st place, 1 point for 2nd place. This includes WAR NWR STP POP HAP PRD CTS GWR and TEC (see the section on stats in "TURN RESULTS"). Ties share the rank.

Every race is ranked, human players and NPRs alike, and any race can win: an NPR that reaches the win target first takes the victory like a player. You see only your own ranks (in the Report and Stats tabs), but they are your place among all the races in the game.

One exception: when the host plays a race in a game with another human player, the host's race can't win, gets no VP for the statistics and isn't ranked (see "THE HOST" in "PLAYING IN YOUR BROWSER"). The victory check passes over it, and the statistics are ranked among the other races only.

## THE GAMEBOARD

The gameboard is a square grid: one hundred by one hundred in 12- and 16-empire games, one hundred and twenty-five by one hundred and twenty-five in 20- and 25-empire games, seventy-five by seventy-five in 8- and 9-empire games, and fifty by fifty in 4-empire games. Things always end turns on grid points. The game board wraps in both directions, so on the 100 board you can move 0,0 to 0,99 or 99,99 or 99,0 (on the 125 board, 0,124 or 124,124 or 124,0; on the 75 board, 0,74 or 74,74 or 74,0; on the 50 board, 0,49 or 49,49 or 49,0).

Movement and scans are square, more for your convenience than anything else. So a ship at 5,5 with a 3 scan and 3 move will detect anything and can move anywhere in the box defined by 2,2 2,8 8,2 8,8 (MO).

The board is made of home squares ("bins") of 25 by 25, each holding about eleven star systems: a home system and its neighbourhood. The host picks the size of the game when creating it:

| Empires | Bins | Board | Systems | Open territory |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4 | 2 by 2 | 50 by 50 | 44 | none (four quadrants) |
| 8 (the default) | 3 by 3 | 75 by 75 | 99 | the centre bin has no home |
| 9 | 3 by 3 | 75 by 75 | 99 | none |
| 12 | 4 by 4 | 100 by 100 | 176 | the four centre bins have no homes |
| 16 | 4 by 4 | 100 by 100 | 176 | none (the classic game) |
| 20 | 5 by 5 | 125 by 125 | 275 | five bins with no homes, one in every row and column, a knight's move apart |
| 25 | 5 by 5 | 125 by 125 | 275 | none (the largest galaxy) |

A bin with no home is open territory: it has its full share of unowned systems, with a very habitable, metal- and agriculture-rich world at its heart, waiting for whoever gets there first. Human players' homes are spread as far apart as the board allows. Homes are always at least 15 squares apart, well outside each other's starting scans; on the 4-empire board every quadrant borders the other three (in both directions, as the board wraps), so your neighbours are 15 to 25 squares away. In the 20-empire game the five open bins are placed so that everyone's surroundings are the same: of the eight bins around yours (counting diagonals, and across the edges, as the board wraps), exactly two are open territory, one beside you and one at a corner, and no two open bins touch. Seats not taken by human players are NPRs, so "empires" means humans and NPRs together. The victory conditions are the same at every size.

## NON PLAYER RACES (NPRs)

There is a "generic" Non Player Race (NPR) that represents a small confederation of small systems. They are like Player Races: they are ranked in the statistics with everyone else (see the "TURN RESULTS" section), and they can win the game. Other races may be NPRs as well; typically, these are seats no human player took, or players that have resigned from the game (an NPR replacement prevents a sudden easily acquired set of systems for a neighbor to pick up). You will be able to tell what races are NPRs when you have contact (get a long or short range scan) with them. The Races tab lists the races you have contacted and marks each as human or computer.

NPR races are somewhat xenophobic (but not necessarily war-like) and do limited negotiation with other races (including each other). You can trade with NPRs using the Trade Technology With NPR command (TTNPR): you offer one of your technologies in exchange for one of theirs. NPRs sometimes offer you a trade themselves, privately in the chat: they offer only what they think is a fair or good swap for them, never to a race they are at war with, that threatens them or that is running away with the galaxy, and they know your technologies only from what they have learned (spy reports, your own trade offers and gifts); otherwise they offer theirs for any technology of yours of a given cost that they lack (see the ANY option of TTNPR). An NPR keeps its word: if you give that exact TTNPR for the turn the offer names, it accepts. NPRs will inform you if you are violating their space, and may take offensive action against your intruding ship. **Bring a warship within one move of an NPR's worlds** (a place from which it could reach one of their systems in a single turn at its speed, or its fleet's speed) **and the NPR declares war on you at once**, unless it is allied with you; a warship at one of your own worlds doesn't count. It won't make peace while your warships are still that close; take them away and it may. Its own warships likewise keep out of reach of the worlds of races it is not at war with, unless it means war. They can attack you; whether they do is dependent on how you treat them, distance from them to your systems, and how well defended your systems are. They will make peace when a war goes badly or goes quiet (send them a message asking for peace, then End War with them), and they ally with races that treat them well, especially against whoever is running away with the galaxy. Once they have Decentralized Trading, NPRs set up trade routes (CTT) at the systems of races they are not at war with, you included, the most valuable ones first: a route costs them a transport and the trip, and earns them a quarter of your system's EU production every turn without costing you anything (see "Deny DCT" if you'd rather it did them less good, or eliminate the route with a spy).

NPRs do not cheat. They play under exactly the same rules as you: the same scans, production, maintenance, prices and happiness (their systems can rebel), and they decide only from what their own turn results show them, just as you do. They are simply played by the computer, and they play to win.

Occasionally, players will drop out of the game (RESIGN). In these cases, they are "buffed" up significantly in order to make them more difficult to capture, since NPRs are not as good as human players.

NPRs like to show off. When an NPR settles or captures a system it gives it a name of its own choosing (with an ordinary rename order, a turn or two later; homes that already have a proper name keep it). And NPRs boast in the game-wide chat about their accomplishments: systems settled and captured, battles won, first contacts, big technologies, victory point milestones, wars and peace, and turns when they did remarkably better than the turn before. They only ever boast about what they themselves know, and they never give away positions, fleets or anyone else's numbers. The host chooses how chatty they are: off, quiet (only the big moments) or normal; it can be changed during the game from the Chat tab. If you knew a system by its old name, your orders can still use the name on your map until you see it again.

Systems that rebel (see "POPULATION") go to the independent rebels, who are NPRs too. The rebels give no orders, pay no maintenance and take no part in diplomacy.

## TECHNOLOGY

You can buy various technologies for different prices in Economic Units (EUs) (PT) (see TECHNOLOGIES section), by receiving a gifted technology from another race (GT), by trading technologies with an NPR (TTNPR), by doing a "Crash Research Program" (CRP), or by capturing another race's system if you are significantly trailing that player in technology. Generally, the format of the technology description is:

`[technology number] technology name [required precursor technologies]` description.

The technology number is used in the order for buying that technology. The required precursor technologies are technology that you must already have in order to buy this technology. You may purchase or be gifted only one technology per turn. Likewise, you may only gift one technology per turn. However, you can institute a Crash Research Program (CRP) to purchase a technology at twice normal cost if you want to get another technology the same turn; each Crash Research Program buys one more technology at twice cost, and you may run more than one in a turn. Technologies that are researched with CRP can be gifted like any other technology. Future Technology is the exception: no more than one Future Technology a turn, however you buy it.

Technology usually has some additional bonus associated with it: better units, better production, or other benefit. Some of these are "artifacts" which can be purchased on a system by system basis (BT). You can also destroy tech artifacts (DT) with the exception of "spies"; for spies, there is a separate order described under "Biomodification Technology," the technology needed to build spies (SRS). You cannot destroy tech or infrastructure (DI) if the system is under attack or interdicted. You can't buy the artifact until you get the technology. There is also a maintenance cost associated with the artifact; you must pay this each turn for each system with an artifact or it is destroyed. Note that it is perfectly acceptable to purchase a technology and an artifact for that technology in the same turn. You must, however, sequence your orders correctly in order to accomplish this. See the "ORDERS" section for some examples.

## ECONOMICS

Economics are based on various types of units, IUs, AUs, and EUs, as described below. Production of these units depends on the characteristics of your individual systems, a topic covered in more detail in "SYSTEMS."

IUs are Industrial Units, a measure of the raw resources that are available. It takes 1 cargo capacity to move up to 20 IUs. IUs are stockpiled on a system basis. They can, however, be moved into "supply depots" (SISD). IUs that were moved into supply depots enter the military storage and distribution system, where dedicated clerks send them to almost, but not quite, the worst possible place to support your units. Somehow, thanks to Non-Commissioned Officers, it all works out and your ships remain in operable condition.

AUs are Agricultural / Luxury units, a measure of consumer goods available for your population. Well-fed people are happy people. Happy people like to have children, foolishly believing the galaxy to be a warm and friendly place in which to raise them. It takes 1 cargo capacity to move up to 20 AUs. AUs are stockpiled on a system by system basis.

EUs are Economic Units. A measure of the economy, tax base, etc. EUs can be shifted between systems at will. Thus only a stockpile for the empire is shown.

EUs, unlike AUs and IUs, can also be gathered from trade routes with other players with the appropriate technology, Decentralized Trading. DCT allows you to gather up to 25% of another player's EU production from systems you have established trade routes with. This is limited to no more than 50% of the total system production, so if three players establish trade routes to a single system, each player begins to receive less trade EUs. It is also affected by a setting by the system owner that can reduce the effectiveness of your route to as little as 25% of normal (see "Deny DCT" order). You can also automatically "kick back" some portion of your proceeds, from 0 to 100%, to the system owner with the "Split DCT" order.

IF is Infrastructure, and represents roads, bridges, hospitals, Inter-spatial Hyperwarp Junctions, and all the other things that make life easier for the average working joe, and therefore make him more productive. It costs ten IUs per point of Infrastructure (BI) you build. Generally, 1 point of Infrastructure doubles the production of one billion population each turn. Having more Infrastructure than population doesn't help with production (empty highways are not particularly useful). You can also destroy infrastructure (DI). Since Infrastructure is built to last, you have to pay to destroy it: 5 EUs per IF destroyed. As with Destroy Technology, you can only do this to a system with normal status.

## SYSTEMS

Systems can scan ships out to four squares away (more with special technology: six with Extended System Scans, ten with Extremely Extended System Scans). Systems have a habitability value that represents the closeness of the environment to home and the amount of habitable land. The habitability value is displayed as Extremely Habitable [EH], Very Habitable [VH], Habitable [H], Poorly Habitable [PH], or Marginally Habitable [MH]. Marginally Habitable and Poorly Habitable systems are hard to settle successfully without advanced technology (you will find it hard to keep a large population happy; a small population will not produce much for you).

Systems normally produce 5 units per point of population; the distribution of these units is controlled by a system profile, described below. Systems may be Metal Rich [MR] (doubles normal IU production), Agriculture Rich [AR] (doubles normal AU production), both [2R], or normal.

Systems have System and AU profiles. The system profile specifies the division of effort in producing AUs, IUs, and EUs (by percentage, adding up to 100%, each a minimum of 10%) (CSP). The AU profile controls distribution of AUs to the population (this is described in more detail below). Systems also have IU and AU stockpiles. Systems may have various artifacts, depending on technology levels.

Default values are 50 25 25 for the System Profile and 33 for the AU profile.

With the System and AU profile, the concept is that you, like the Fed, encourage or discourage activity in certain markets by indirect controls (CSP CAP CAPN). You can influence, but not totally control, planetary production with these profiles. Sure, you could ship in AUs to your MMR (mega-metal-rich) systems (no, they do not exist), but the lettuce is all wilty and the Spam... well, it's Spam. So some people are going to grow food even though the market is saturated. This also has the side effect of making MR and AR systems more balanced.

Population growth is not something you set directly. (Earlier editions had a Growth Rate profile, CGR, for convincing the population to breed like bunnies or causing makin' bacon to slow to a crawl; the web game has no such order, and every system breeds at the people's basic desire to breed.) Basic population growth is dependent on how happy the people are... if too depressed, they get off by slapping their spouses around rather than making whoopee. It is also influenced by population, starports, system habitability, technology, and system government (i.e., leaders... see "LEADERS" section).

Systems can have disasters befall them. The effects of these disasters will be described in your turn results (see "TURN RESULTS" section).

Systems have a "happiness factor". This represents the attitude of the population. This is displayed as Extremely Happy [EH], Very Happy [VH], Happy [HA], Not Happy [NH], Unhappy [UH], or Rebellion [RB]. If the system is in rebellion, the population will secede from your empire. If they are unhappy or not happy, minor mayhem and sabotage typically result, with an occasional rebel NPR militia unit appearing on the scene. If they are Very Happy or Extremely Happy, they may turn out to show their appreciation for your glorious empire with some token of gratitude.

Happiness is affected by spending AUs. Systems have an AU spending profile that specifies the percentage of the AU stockpile to be released each turn (in other words, your system profile determines the number of AUs you produce and place on your stockpile; your AU profile determines the number of AUs you take out of your stockpile and give to the population) (CAP)(CAPN).

There are technologies that affect happiness; these include Extended Home Computing (EHC) and Cyberspace (CS) which improve happiness directly, and planetary defensive technologies that let people sleep more securely at night (Planetary Defenses, Spacewarp Weapons, Advanced Planetary Defenses). See the "TECHNOLOGIES" section.

To put it all together, suppose you have a system with the default System Profile and 4 population, with no technology, leader or MR/AR/2R bonuses. They would produce 10 AUs, 5 IUs, and 5 EUs. With the default AU profile of 33%, 3 AUs would be fed to the population and 7 would remain in the AU stockpile; with an AU profile of 100%, all ten AUs would be fed to the population; if the AU profile was 10%, 1 AU would be fed to the population and 9 would remain in the AU stockpile (note that fractions are truncated; this is true of the System Profile as well).

Systems can be in one of three statuses: Normal, Under Attack, or Interdicted. Systems in Under Attack status produce at half normal rate and pay twice as many IUs and EUs to build CTs, ships, and technological artifacts. They also cannot destroy technology, destroy infrastructure, or shift IUs to a supply depot. In addition to the other restrictions mentioned above, systems that are interdicted do not produce, and cannot build. See PLACING SYSTEMS IN UNDER ATTACK OR INTERDICTED STATUS for information on how systems change status.

Landing 1 or more population on an unpopulated system makes it your own (the system must be unowned, or an empty system of your own; an empty system still held by another empire's troops has to be taken by ground assault). You can immediately purchase infrastructure, etc., if you unload IUs the same turn. Unload the population first: until it lands the system isn't yours, so an earlier IU or AU unload fails. In the ship panel, "Claim this system (land population)" adds the orders in the right order. By default the CSP ratios are 50% AU / 25% IU / 25% EU. If two races attempt to land at the same system, the person landing first gets the system. Who lands first is determined randomly. The second landing order will fail (unless a "UG" order is specified; see SHIP ORDERS for details). Systems normally have a limit of 10 billion population which they can support without additional technology (BT EUS).

A system whose population falls below half a billion is emptied: its stockpiles, infrastructure, starport and artifacts are lost. If none of your troops remain on it, it returns to unowned space. You may also give up a system of 1 population or less yourself (SR); it is emptied the same way.

## STARPORTS

Systems also have a starport at which ships and Combat Troops (CTs) are built, and which improves the general productivity of the system by promoting trade. Starports have an integer rating that indicates how many ships or CTs it can build. Starport rating is expressed as class 1, class 2, etc. One ship and two CTs can be built for each starport rating point (e.g., a class 2 starport can build 2 ships, 1 ship and 2 CTs, or 4 CTs). Militia count as CTs here. Salvaging a ship (SS) or a CT (SCT) also uses one build. You may build or upgrade starports (BSP) at the following cost:

```
25 * 2 ^ (starport rating - 1) IUs and EUs
```

So a class 1 starport costs 25 IUs and 25 EUs, a class 2 costs 50 IUs and 50 EUs, a class 3 100/100, a class 4 200/200, etc.

Starports improve system productivity by 10% per rating; on a system with 4 population, no technology or other enhancements, it would produce 22 "units" of goods instead of 20.

If you upgrade a starport, you pay the delta cost between the one you have and the one you are purchasing. Thus upgrading from a class 3 to a class 4 will cost you 100/100 IUs/EUs. You can only upgrade a starport by one class a turn.

You may not build at a starport that you have already built or upgraded that turn, so be a little cautious about suddenly maxing out your building capability in the face of an oncoming horde.

You may also destroy starports (DSP). Since these things are built to last, it is difficult and expensive to destroy one. You may reduce a starport's rating by 1 each turn, at a cost of 10 EUs per turn.

## SHIPS

Ships in the game represent exploration, transport, or war vessels directly controlled by the empire. There is an underlying assumption in the game that civilian commercial space traffic is also occurring, but it is transparent to game play. This mechanism is how spies, leaders, and supply depot IUs are moved from system to system. But in all of these cases, there is a standard movement rate or assumed pre-planned distribution / movement rather than an actual vessel.

The ships you do control traverse the interstellar spaces. They have five factors, Attack, Defense, Scan, Movement, and Cargo Capacity. They require maintenance costs be paid out of the empire's EU stockpile and IU supply depots. If the empire cannot pay the costs out of its supplies, the ship is destroyed. Normal maintenance is 5 IUs and 5 EUs a turn. IU maintenance costs are double if the ship is not within 4 turns of a system owned by the empire (actual distance varies with movement rate) except for scout class (Scout, SuperScout, MegaScout) ships, which just pay the normal maintenance fees. IU and EU costs are half, rounded down, if the ship is at an empire-owned system. Ships have one of three postures: hostile [H], neutral [N], or friendly [F] (SP). If more than one ship is present, all ships will take the posture of the most aggressive ship (i.e., hostile followed by neutral followed by friendly). Ships from different races interact as follows:

| Ship 2 \ Ship 1 | Hostile | Neutral | Friendly |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Hostile** | Attack each other | Attack each other | Attack each other\* |
| **Neutral** | Attack each other | 50% chance of attack | 10% chance of attack |
| **Friendly** | Attack each other\* | 10% chance of attack | Do not attack each other |

\* Hostile ships fire first, then friendly ships fire (i.e., they lose initiative)

Where there is a chance of attack, it is a chance that the two sides attack each other.

Additionally, ships that are hostile will attack systems as specified in the combat rules given later. Ships that are neutral or friendly will not attack (or interdict) systems. Transports automatically assume a hostile posture if the Land Combat Troops order (LACT) is used for ground assault in a system unless the owner is allied with the empire owning the system. Since this will cause all of your ships in the square to behave as if they have a hostile posture, using the Land Combat Troops order will automatically cause an all out attack on the system. Note the transport's posture really changes. If you want it to subsequently shift to a friendly posture, you must use the Ship's Posture (SP) order.

Ship's posture and effects are mitigated as follows by relations between empires. Neutral empires are as described in the paragraphs above. Allied empires do not attack each other. Empires at war always attack each other. See "ALLIANCES" for more information on forming and breaking alliances.

Ships also have a combat role (CSCR) that is significant when engaging in ground combat. A ship may have a Space Combat [SC], Multi-Role [MR], Ground Combat [GC], or a Raid [RD] role. See COMBAT for a description of how these roles affect combat. New ships start in Space Combat, except transports (Transport and MassTransit, including the Transport you start with), which start in Ground Combat; armed cargo ships such as the Armed Transport and Multirole CS are warships and start in Space Combat. Change a ship's role with CSCR, or give a role in the BS order.

Transports and other ship classes that have cargo carrying capacity may also load and unload AUs, IUs, and population with a Load (L), Unload (U), or Unload Gift (UG) order, and load or land Combat Troops with a Load (LOCT), Land Combat Troop (LACT), or Drop Combat Troop (DOCT) order (see COMBAT TROOPS and COMBAT sections for details on landing ground troops). Loading puts things on transports, while unloading removes them from the Transport and places them on a system. Loading and Unloading can only be done at a system you own, and the system must be in the same square as the Transport. There is an alternate Unload order, Unload Gift (UG), which gives things to the system you are at regardless of who owns it. There is also a System Load order (SL), which loads a transport from a system's stockpiles; this order allows you to give AUs, IUs, or population to another player by loading them on his transport (if he tried to load them with a standard load command, the load would fail since he doesn't own the system). You can also give away a whole ship, with everything on it (GS).

One exception to the above is landing population in an uninhabited system, which will successfully unload the population and which causes it to join your empire.

Landing population on a system that already has people costs happiness, more so the more crowded the system is and the poorer its habitability (Extended Home Computing and Cloud Seeding soften it). A large enough landing gets the message "Crowds protest in anger over forced immigration", but still goes ahead. A landing that would push the system's happiness below zero (into rebellion) is refused instead: the population stays on the ship, and the order result tells you to land fewer. Check orders shows this before you submit.

Landing combat troops on a system inhabited by another race with whom you are not allied causes a ground assault on that system. Landing combat troops at an allied system places your combat troops on the system in support of the allied empire, but the troops remain owned by you.

There are a few tricks to this. You capture a system as soon as you unload your population onto it. Therefore, you can change the system's System and AU profiles using the CSP, CAP and CAPN orders or its name with the NSY order (see "ORDERS") only AFTER you unload your population (since the system is not owned by you until that point). You have to sequence your orders properly in order to accomplish this.

Ships may stand, move to a x,y location (MO), move to a system you have seen (MS), intercept another ship (I), or "Guard" (G) which allows a ship to move toward scanned ships in its range. Interception is useful to get intruding enemy ships prior to them reaching your systems. Guard is a good way to nuke those irritating scouts that like to buzz the outside of your empire.

You begin the game with the ship classes shown below (showing [attack, defense, scan, move, cargo] in the brackets). Other ship classes become available with additional technology (BS). A full list of ship classes is provided in the "CHARTS" section at the end of the rules.

```
Scout [0/1/3/4/0]     Cost 20 IU, 20 EU
Transport [0/1/1/4/3] Cost 20 IU, 20 EU
Frigate [1/2/2/4/0]   Cost 30 IU, 30 EU
```

Ship maintenance costs vary as specified in the rules above.

Older ships can be salvaged (SS) at a starport on a system that is in "NORMAL" status in order to recover 75% of the IU and EU cost of building the ship (but obviously not the maintenance costs you have spent on it).

## COMBAT TROOPS

Combat Troops (CTs) hold systems. They have two factors: Attack and Defense. Each ground troop requires one cargo capacity to transport. Troops also have a posture: Attack [A], Defend [D], Entrenched [E], or entrenching [1/2/3].

All troops can move between Attack and Defend postures at any time. If in defend mode, they add 50% (rounded up) to defensive capability but have NO attack. Infantry troops can also entrench. Whether a Combat Troop is an Infantry Unit or not is indicated later in the rules. When an infantry unit is Entrenched, its defense is doubled and its attack is one half rounded down. Additionally, its defense against ground bombardment (described below) is quadrupled or four times normal defense. It takes three turns to entrench a unit; while entrenching, the CT has a normal defense and no attack. A unit that is attacked while it is still digging in goes back to Attack posture, and ordering a unit that is already entrenching or entrenched to entrench again doesn't restart the count.

Combat Troops have a gravity well disadvantage, and so can be crushed by space-to-ground fire (see "COMBAT"). Note that Combat Troops land (LACT) before ship movement and combat, so the transports carrying them must have been at the system you are attacking (and survived combat) the turn before. However, you may also drop combat troops in one-use "drop pods" (DOCT) which lands them at the end of movement with some chance (30%) of the troop burning up during the drop. The chance of getting smoked increases by 15% each for PD, SW, and APD installations on the ground (so a system with all three burns up 75% of the drops).

Combat Troops can be salvaged (SCT) at a starport on a system you own in normal status to recover 50% of the original cost of the unit.

You begin the game with the following types of units available (other types become available with technology) (BCU).

**Militia.** [1/1] Militia cannot be transported. They cost 5 IU and 5 EU to build, and 2 EU / turn for maintenance. You may not have more Militia than population at a system (any excess is disbanded at maintenance). Militias are NOT infantry units. Militia use starport capacity just like other CTs (two per starport class).

**Shock troops.** [2/2] Shock Troops cost 10 IU and 10 EUs to build, and 2 EU / turn maintenance. Shock Troops are infantry units.

## LEADERS

Leaders are individuals that have come to power in your empire. They function as system governors or Admirals of a fleet. They have a significant effect on combat, disaster recovery, happiness, economic growth, and other things. This effect can be positive or negative (but is generally positive). You will get occasional reports concerning leaders' capabilities in handling a crisis. These will help you analyze a leader's strengths and weaknesses. You get leaders at random. When you get one, a description of the leader will be provided in your turn results and look something like this:

> Morta - Helsppit Morta graduated from the FORCE:GROUND Center for Tactical Studies in the top of her class. She was awarded the Distinguished SimNet Battleplay medal for her unorthodox approach in the Agincourt Milsim. She is reputed to be brilliant, friendly, and solidly grounded in the scientific disciplines. A solid, reliable manager with an eye for detail, she is a very impressive young woman.

However, you can also do a "Leader Report" (LR) if you like raw numeric stats; the Leaders tab shows them for every leader anyway.

When you get a leader, you deploy him or her with a "Move Leader" command (ML) or "Move Leader to Fleet" command (MLF). You are not forced to do this; if you do not, the leader will float in limbo forever until (s)he dies of natural causes. The leader is in transit for two turns and arrives on the third: a leader ordered to a system on turn 10 takes up the post on turn 12. You may also move a leader to an administrative position if they have a negative effect on a system (PLOI).

Leaders do age. For a while, they will improve with time (how much is dependent on their intelligence). After a while, they begin to degrade (again, degradation rate is dependent on intelligence). Leaders also die of natural and unnatural causes. Natural causes include death during combat (see "FLEETS"); for unnatural, see the "TECHNOLOGIES" section on Biomodification technology. After ten turns, there is a small chance of leader death that grows with leader age.

You can get a report on your leaders using the "Leader Report" (LR) command. This report shows five ratings for each leader: Management which affects production; Social, which affects system happiness; Combat which affects combat; Engineering which affects random events, and Intelligence which indicates how the leader will improve over time. The ratings go from 0 to 99, with a rating of 50 meaning the leader will have a neutral effect. The report also shows the leader's age (number of turns in the game), which affects degradation and chance of death.

## POPULATION

Your people, measured in billions (one population point = one billion people). It takes one cargo capacity to move one billion people (in the form of frozen embryos with sufficient support staff and equipment to convert them to useful cannon fodder on a distant system). Population require food and luxury goods (AUs) to grow and to be happy. Unhappy people begin to create minor rebellions, and very unhappy people will cause the entire planet to rebel. Happiness is affected by capture and/or loss of systems, habitability, overcrowding, AUs spent (AU stockpile times AU profile, or numeric value... see system orders), leaders, combat situation, and certain technological advances.

Systems that are UNHAPPY have a 50% chance of some kind of disgruntled population show of force. This is usually destruction of public goods (IUs or IF), but one in ten times is a NPR militia unit. Under these circumstances, you end up in ground combat. If the NPR militia unit is the only unit on the system at the end of the turn (the obvious default if you have no combat troops on the system), the system is "captured" by the NPRs, just as if you had a ground assault by a NPR transport.

Systems that are NOT HAPPY have a 10% chance of a minor proletariat uprising.

Systems that are Very Happy have a 10% chance of a "I love the emperor day," which has various random but beneficial results.

Systems that are Extremely Happy have a 50% chance of a "I love the emperor" day.

Systems that are in REBELLION automatically become NPRs (the independent rebels), along with all combat troops on the system.

There is one exception to these rules; they do not apply if the system is UNDER ATTACK or INTERDICTED.

Under these circumstances, systems that are UNHAPPY act as loyal little slaves. Systems that are in REBELLION have a ten percent chance of joining the attackers.

You should note that the rules specify that UNDER ATTACK and INTERDICTED status have an EFFECT on morale. They do not automatically put the system in rebellion. So assault by Combat Troops may be the only quick way to take the system.

## ALLIANCES

You can ally/declare war, send EUs, gift technology, or spy on any race you have made contact with (had a short range or long range scan of) from the turn you make contact on.

Alliances allow you to override reactions for ship postures for specific races (DA). Likewise, declaring war does the same (DW). These overrides are described in the "SHIPS" section of the rules. There is also a neutral posture (EWA). Declaring war automatically declares war for both races. Declaring an alliance declares an alliance from YOU... the other race will only become allied if THEY submit the same order. If you submit a DA (Declare Alliance) order but he does not, YOUR ships are treated as having a "FRIENDLY" posture, while his are whatever they are set at. If they are hostile, interaction occurs as if they were hostile and you were friendly regardless of your ship posture. Note that there is no way to determine whether the other player has ordered an alliance with you... it only shows up in his turn results. Trust is a difficult thing, isn't it?

The various states you can be in and the results associated with submitting the Declare Alliance, Declare War, or End War/Alliance orders are shown in the chart below.

Status, You / Them (N = Neutral, A = allied, W = At War, E = End War)

| Order | N/N | N/A | A/N | A/A | W/W | W/E |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| DA | A/N | A/A | Fails | Fails | Fails | Fails |
| DW | W/W | W/W | W/W | W/W | Fails | Fails |
| EWA | Fails | Fails | N/N | N/A | E/W | N/N |

At the end of the order turn, any End War statuses are converted back to "war." In other words, if you and the other player do not both declare an end to the war the same turn, you both remain at war.

If you are both allied, your fleets combine in actions against enemy fleets and Combat Troops. See COMBAT for more details.

Say your ships are in "Friendly" mode, and you encounter a ship in "Hostile" mode from a race that you are at war with at the same time you encounter a ship from a third race that is also in friendly mode. The two races in "friendly" mode ignore each other. The races that are at war are treated as if the ships were in "hostile" mode with one another (i.e., you do not lose initiative because your ship is in friendly mode and the other ship is in hostile mode) and attack each other.

## COMBAT

There is an example at the end of this section. That should make things clearer.

A combat happens on any square where ships of different empires meet, where a ship is at another empire's populated system, or where hostile troops share a system. Everyone present gets the combat report.

### SHIP COMBAT

Ship to Ground: Depends on combat role assigned as follows:

**Space Combat [SC]:** Attack any spaceships as long as some are left. If no enemy ships are left, do not attack.

**Multi-Role [MR]:** As Space Combat, except that if no enemy spaceships are present at the beginning of combat, default to Ground Combat role. The ship's attack factor, however, is halved when used for attacking ground troops since it's firing from a higher orbit.

**Ground Combat [GC]:** Ships not engaged by enemy spacecraft attack Combat Troops. Attack factors from all ships in this mode are combined. Each 4 points of attack eliminates 1 point of effective Combat Troop defense per turn (note that Combat Troop effective defense may be higher than normal defense depending on the Combat Troop's posture). If insufficient attack points exist to eliminate a unit, they have an attack point / (5 \* defensive capability) chance of destroying the unit. Attacks are allocated randomly to units until all attack points are allocated. Ship attacks occur prior to ground attacks. If no ground combat units are left, the ship does not fire. Ground troops have a small (25%) chance of escaping ground bombardment by hiding in caves. In this case they detract from the space attack but the unit survives. Ships destroyed in the space combat don't bombard.

**Raid [RD]:** Ships not engaged by enemy spacecraft attack civilian targets. Each five points of attack eliminates one point of infrastructure, some part of an artifact, 10 IUs out of the IU stockpile or 10 AUs out of the AU stockpile (determined randomly). Population is eliminated by one billion people for every 25 attack factors used in the attack. If insufficient attack points exist to destroy an artifact or population, there is an attack point \* 2 / total cost of artifact chance of destroying the artifact and an attack point / 25 chance of destroying 1 population point. Some attack points may be wasted shooting at non-existent targets (pounding the rubble, so to speak).

Planetary Defenses and Advanced Planetary Defenses each halve the effect of bombardment on the system's Combat Troops, and Advanced Planetary Defenses also halve raids (see the technology descriptions).

Ship to ship: Simultaneous. The algorithm is as follows:

```
Sum all attacks in SC, MR, GC, and RD modes;
Calculate Mixed Fleet Bonus (described below);
Determine defense of all races you are attacking;
If total defense is greater than 0 MR attacks are added to SC;
If SC less than total defense, GC ships are shifted to SC,
        then RD ships, one ship at a time in random
        order, until SC greater than or equal to total defense
        or all ships are in effective SC mode;
Effective attack = sqrt(random(1-SC^2));
Ships owned by empires you are attacking
        are eliminated in order of Combat Role (SC, then
        MR, then GC, then RD) within empire (and randomly
        within role), one at a time, until SC less than next
        ship's defense (each SC point eliminating a defensive
        point).
If [random(1-100) less than remaining SC / ship's defense * 100]
        ship is destroyed;
```

If ships are forced to move from GC or RD roles to SC, they only get 1/2 of their normal attack. MR ships left over for the ground attack (when there were no enemy ships) attack at half, as described above.

Planetary Defenses, Spacewarp Weapons and Advanced Planetary Defenses join the space battle on the owner's side: PD subtracts 1 and SW subtracts 5 from the attack of each hostile empire, and SW (1 attack) and APD (5 attack) fire at the attackers.

Mixed Fleet Bonus (MFB) reflects the tactical advantage if you have a mix of ships of different base types. Ship base types (lines) are STANDARD, OFFENSIVE, DEFENSIVE and FAST; the line of every ship class is shown in the ship chart in "CHARTS".

MFB is calculated as follows. If you have attack points in all four ship lines, those attack factors are multiplied by 3. If you have three ship lines, attack factors are multiplied by 2. With 2 ships lines, they are multiplied by 1.5. MFB multiplies attack only; it never adds to defense.

For instance, if you have one FF, one F, one Patroller, and one Striker, with 1 / 1 / 2 / 5 attack, the first attack point for each ship would be multiplied by three since you have at least one attack factor in all four lines, the second attack factor (for the Patroller and Striker) would be multiplied by 1.5 since you have two ships lines, and the rest of the attack factors would be normal, giving you an 18 attack instead of a 9 attack. MFB doesn't help with ground combat. MFB stacks with leader bonuses.

After all attacks are completed, the ships are actually "destroyed" (i.e., the order in which this happens doesn't keep a ship from "firing back").

Note that the effective attack is not sqrt(SC^2), or SC; this function provides a very different distribution that is weighed heavily toward the higher end of the 1 - SC scale. For instance, assume a total of 10 points of attack in SC mode; 10^2 = 100. A random number from 1 to 100 is selected, say 50. The square root of 50 is taken, and you have an effective 7 attack. You will always have at least a one attack.

The only exception to the above is attacks with initiative, which are done first, destroying ships AND removing the ship's attack from the owner's SC, MR, GC, or RD points (whatever the ship's effective role is).

The ship's effective role doesn't change the ordered ship's posture; next turn, the ship will return to the previously set combat role (but obviously may be overridden again).

Allied empires never fire on each other, and allied ships' defense counts toward a system's status (see below).

### FLEETS

A Fleet provides a way to help administrate your ships when their numbers are getting large enough to be a burden. Additionally, a fleet allows you to use a leader to enhance the attack and defense of the ships traveling with the fleet (note that some ships may be members of the fleet without traveling with the fleet; see rules below). Fleets provide a method of giving orders to a large number of ships with a single command, including movement, loading and unloading cargo, setting posture, and setting combat role. Fleets also "roll up" a number of ships for reporting purposes into a single line.

An admiral risks his neck along with his ships: after each battle, the admiral is killed with a chance of 25% times the fraction of the fleet's ships lost in that battle (so an admiral whose whole fleet is wiped out dies one time in four). A governor is not safe either; he dies when his system is captured.

You form a Fleet with a "Create Fleet" command (CF), providing a fleet name used to reference the fleet from that point on (for you and the other players). Ships are added to the fleet with the "Add ship to Fleet" (AF) command. Ships that have not joined the main body of the fleet at the end of the turn move to intercept the fleet and are reported separately with your ships. You can remove a ship from the fleet with the Leave Fleet (LF) command, or by adding it to a different fleet. Two of your fleets on the same square can be combined with Merge Fleets (MGF): every ship of the first joins the second, and the first is disbanded.

Movement for Fleets is very similar to that for ships, in that you may move to a X, Y location (MF), move to a star system (MFS), or intercept a ship (FIS). Unlike ships, there is no "Guard" order for fleets.

The only real difference is speed (FS). A fleet may be set for automatic speed [A], or as a specific numeric speed from 1 to 9. In "Automatic," the Fleet moves at the speed of the slowest ship in the main fleet body. If you set a speed, the Fleet will move at that speed (but no faster than its fastest ship present), leaving any slower ships behind to chase after it. Speed is ignored for intercept situations; ships move at maximum speed to intercept other fleets or ships.

Setting postures (CFP) for a fleet includes the standard ship postures (Hostile, Neutral, and Friendly) [H, N, F]. Setting the Fleet posture to one of these changes all ships in the fleet to that posture. There is also a Manual [M] setting that leaves the ships in whatever posture they were previously set, an Automatic Hostile [AH] posture that sets all warships (ships with 1 or better attack factors) to Hostile and all non-warships to Friendly, and an Automatic Neutral [AN] posture that sets warships to Neutral and non-warships to Friendly.

As with postures, you may set a Fleet's combat role (CFCR) using the standard roles [SC, MR, GC, RD] or Manual [M]. The other options are to assume an Offensive [OF], Defensive [DF], Protective [PT], or Roll Your Own [RO] role. Offensive is best for system assault, Defensive for system defense, Protective for defending smaller craft from an attacker, and Roll Your Own for special cases. These roles result in the following:

- **OF** - Transports carrying CTs RD, non-attack ships SC, everyone else MR
- **DF** - All non-attacks SC, everyone else MR
- **PT** - All non-attacks RD, everyone else SC
- **RO** - You specify number of GC attack pts, number of RD attack pts, and combat role for non-attackers. All non GC/RD warships shift to MR, all Combat Troop carrying transports to RD.

The fleet's role is applied to the member ships when it is set and whenever ships join the fleet.

**A fleet with no ships left is disbanded.** At the very end of the turn, after combat and everything else, any fleet that has none of your ships left (all destroyed, salvaged, disbanded, gifted or moved out) is disbanded, just as if you had given DF, and your results say "Fleet X has no ships left and was disbanded." Its name is free again, so you can make a new fleet with it. Its admiral, if he survived the battle, and a leader still on the way to it, go back to awaiting assignment. A fleet you create this turn is safe until it gets its ships: after Create fleet (CF) the Add to fleet orders fill it, and a Create fleet at X,Y (CFXY) collects its ships at the end of the turn. If such a new fleet still has no ships at the end of its first turn (for example, nothing was on the CFXY square), you are told, and you have the next turn to add ships to it (AF or AFXY); if it is still empty at the end of that turn it is disbanded.

See the ORDERS section for a description of fleet orders, and the TURN RESULTS section for a description of fleet reports.

### GROUND COMBAT

Ground combat is run using the same basic algorithm as space combat, except all combat is simultaneous since there is no hostile / neutral / friendly posture for ground units. Ground attack occurs after ship combat, so units burned off from space are eliminated prior to this phase. Combat Troops from two empires may only be on a single system without attacking one another if both empires are allied with each other. If the alliance is terminated, the Combat Troops will attack that turn.

After a battle with troops on the ground, the battle report (in the Report tab, in the selection panel for that square, and in the classic text report) has a **Ground forces after the battle** block: for each side in the battle, its troops by type, before → after. Only the races in the battle get its report. Selecting the system on the map also lists the troops on the ground now: yours wherever they are, other races' on your own systems, and elsewhere only when you have a short range scan of the system this turn.

### PLACING SYSTEMS IN UNDER ATTACK OR INTERDICTED STATUS

After all combat is resolved, the system's status is determined. Systems are in the Normal status if they are not under attack by a ship or ships with at least one attack point and at least one third as many attack points as you have defensive points in your and allied empire ships and / or artifacts (Planetary Defenses 1, Spacewarp Weapons 5, Advanced Planetary Defenses 15) at the star system. The enemy ship/ships must be set to attack the system (see "SHIPS"); that is, have a ship that is Hostile, a fleet of ships of which one is hostile, a fleet of ships where a Transport lands combat troops for a ground attack, or a ship or fleet of ships owned by an empire at war with you. The system is also under attack if non-owner, non-allied empire combat troops are in the system (for instance, the rebel militias that can pop up on systems with low happiness) and have at least one third the defense points you have in your and allied empire Combat Troops.

If the system is under attack, but total attack factors of enemy ships in the system do not exceed three times the total defensive factors of friendly ships in the system (owner or allied empire ships), the system is in Under Attack status. If the total attack factor is more than three times the defense factors, the system is Interdicted (note that a system with no ships is automatically interdicted). A system with enemy Combat Troops with attack points greater than or equal to the number of defensive points of empire or allied empire Combat Troops is also in Interdicted status. See "Systems" for a description of the effects of Under Attack and Interdicted Status on the system.

Systems only change by one category a turn; thus, a "normal" system that is suddenly attacked by a ship with no defending ships will move to "under attack" status the turn it is attacked, then to "interdicted" status the next turn. Likewise, an interdicted system will change to "under attack" one turn and "normal" the next.

### CAPTURING SYSTEMS

If all indigenous ground combat troops are eliminated and the attacker has at least one ground combat troop remaining, the system becomes the property of the attacker. Note that this means that the attacker can have his troops posture as defensive (defense increased by 50%, but they don't attack) and still take over the system if there are no other troops there. If troops of several attacking empires survive, the one with the largest surviving troop strength (attack plus defense) gets the system.

Of course, sometimes those scuds with the nasty biological payload wander a bit off course and hit a major population center, or a nuke takes out a bit of industrial complex near the troops you intended to vaporize, but you can't make an omelet without breaking eggs. As a result, system capture also results in the destruction of all stockpiles, the death of a Governor (if the system has one; any other leader posted there loses the post), a 10-50% population loss, and some chance of the destruction of Artifacts and Infrastructure.

Additionally, the planet may rebel and join the attacker if the population is very unhappy. Poor habitability, low food supply, and heavily outnumbered troops or spacecraft affect happiness. Since rebellion results in a "friendly" takeover, the results are less drastic to the civilian population. If the planet rebels, all stockpiles are halved and the governor is either killed (75% chance) or joins the enemy.

System capture is a major media event, played over and over again for your entire race. It therefore has an effect on everyone's morale. System capture results in a burst of patriotic fervor that increases happiness. System loss results in a burst of dissident dissatisfaction and marches on the capital demanding the beheading of the emperor (good thing you own all the guns and simply slag the saps) as well as lowered happiness.

Finally, capturing a system from an opposing player with better technology than you results in a chance of capturing a technology from them. "Better" means they own more than three technologies more than you do; the technology captured is one of their six most advanced.

### COMBAT EXAMPLE

The Porkines move into a system owned by the Groachi with two destroyers, two frigates, and a transport with 3 jump troops. The ships have the following characteristics:

| Ship | Posture | Combat Role |
|---|---|---|
| Destroyer 1 | Neutral | Space Combat |
| Destroyer 2 | Neutral | Ground Combat |
| Frigate 1 | Neutral | Ground Combat |
| Frigate 2 | Neutral | Raid |
| Transport 1 | Friendly | Space Combat |

The Groachi have no ships in system, and are not allied or at war with the Porkines. That turn, the Porkines do not attack the Groachi but do get a short range scan. They find the system is metal rich, and submit orders for the transport to land combat troops. The Groachi player, however, has a Frigate and two Scouts within one move of the system and order them to move to the system, just in case. The Groachi ships look like:

| Ship | Posture | Combat Role |
|---|---|---|
| Frigate | Friendly | Space Combat |
| Scout 1 | Friendly | Space Combat |
| Scout 2 | Friendly | Space Combat |

The next turn, landing the combat troops automatically makes the fleet hostile (changing any one ship's posture to hostile would have the same effect).

Space combat comes first. Destroyer 1 and Transport have a combined 3 attack, which is less than the [2 + 1 + 1] defense of the Groachi. As a result, destroyer 2 is shifted to Space Combat, but because it shifted from GC to SC, it is only worth 1/2 normal attack (its 3 attack gives 1, rounded down), for a total of 4. The Groachi forgot to change the posture of their ships, so the Porkines fire first (see the rules on the effect of ship posture on combat). A random roll of 1-4^2 or 1-16 results in a 9; the square root gives the Porkines a 3 attack; the two Scouts are selected first randomly, and destroyed. With 1 attack left against its 2 defense, the Frigate has a 1/2 chance of being destroyed, and a roll determines that it does survive the combat.

Now the Groachi fire back. Note that if the Groachi Frigate had been destroyed, the Groachi, due to loss of initiative, would not be able to return fire. The Frigate has a one attack, which is randomly assigned to the Transport (the Transport and Destroyer 1 being in Space Combat and therefore the first attacked). The Transport is destroyed.

Space combat finished, we turn to the ground attack. Porkine Transport 1 landed three jump troops prior to its destruction. Opposing them are three militia and three shock troops. Space to ground attacks come first, however. Porkine Destroyer 2 is in ground attack mode but had to shift to Space Combat and so has no ground attack. Frigate 1 is in Ground attack, and Frigate 2 is in raid mode. Frigate 1 attacks a militia at random. This militia is in "Defend" mode with an effective defense of 2; the Frigate has a 1 / (5 \* 2) = 1/10 chance of destroying it, and misses. Frigate 2 attacks a tech artifact at random. This system has only one artifact, Extended Home Computing (otherwise the artifact would be selected at random as well). Since EHC costs 75 IUs, the Frigate expends all attack factors for a (1\*2/75) or about 2.7% chance of eliminating the artifact. In total during the attack on the system one attack factor in "Raid" combat mode was used; there is a 1/25 or 4% chance that one billion people were killed, and it is determined that they were.

Now the Combat Troops get into it. Attacks, unlike their space counterpart, are conducted simultaneously, with elimination decided by attack roll and random selection of units. At the end, the Groachi have one militia left. The Porkines have two jump troops. Since the Porkines have more attack factors than the Groachi have defense factors on the ground, the system moves toward "interdicted" status, but since it started at "normal" and only shifts one status per turn, it ends up in "under attack" status this turn.

The Porkines have two Destroyers and two Frigates for a total of 8 attack factors; the Groachi have one Frigate with a 2 defense factor. This provides an attack/defense ratio of 4 to 1, more than the three times the defense needed to interdict the system from space, so the ships push the system toward interdiction as well as the ground troops; it still only moves one step, to "under attack", this turn. (With the original game's Destroyers, 2/4, the Porkines had 6 attack factors, exactly 3 to 1, just short of interdicting from space; see the ship rebalance note in "CHARTS".)

## TURN RESULTS

When a turn has run, the Report tab shows you what happened (see "THE REPORT TAB" for finding your way around it); the standing state of your empire is also in the Empire, Forces, Leaders and Races tabs, and on the map. The turn results contain:

1. **Order results.** Every order you gave, with the reason for any rejection, plus the capture of unowned systems as a result of a pop unload, any technology purchased (the LT order displays all techs owned), any technology gifted to you, and any EUs gifted to you. Gifts say whether they arrived before or after your own orders ran.
2. **Messages** from other races (MESSAGE).
3. **Rebellion and happiness events.** If any of your systems are not happy [NH], unhappy [UH] or in rebellion [RB] and a minor or major rebellion occurs as a result, it is shown here. "I love the emperor day" celebrations are shown here as well.
4. **Maintenance ledger.** Your IU supply depot and EU stockpile at the start of the turn; everything your orders spent (builds, techs, counter-intelligence, farcaster and warp tunnel use, destroying infrastructure or a starport), got back from salvage (SS, SCT), gave away (GE, GT, TTNPR) or received as gifts; EU production and trade; IUs transferred to the supply depot; artifact maintenance; then your supply depot and EU stockpile paying for each ship, Combat Troop and spy, and the end-of-turn totals. The start plus every line gives the end-of-turn totals. IUs that orders take from (or salvage returns to) a system's own stockpile are shown in their own column, since they do not come from the supply depot.
5. **Combat reports**, showing the initial state and final result of any combat your ships or systems were involved in.
6. **Random events.** It should be noted that these effects are done after maintenance costs, so they do not show up in the maintenance ledger and final system totals might be different from what you think.
7. **Staff reports:**
   - **Economy:** the EU stockpile, IU Supply Depot, and EU, AU and IU production for your empire. EU production includes EUs gathered in trade. It doesn't include any changes due to random events.
   - **Statistics:** your rank in each statistic (see below).
   - **Relations** with other empires (the Races tab), where your status (Neutral, Allied or at War with) other races is displayed, along with whether each is a human or a computer race and news of extinct races. A race appears here once you have had a long or short range scan of it.
   - **Leader transit:** leaders on their way to a post, or awaiting assignment (the Leaders tab).
   - **Intelligence:** where each of your spies is, and what it found (or how it died).
   - **Your ships, fleets and systems** (the Forces and Empire tabs, and the selection panel on the map). For ships: the system the ship is at (if any), ship number, type, location, planned move (a destination, an intercept target, Guard, or STAND), posture, combat role, and any cargo. For fleets: location, destination, posture and role (with the extra fleet options), speed, total attack, defense and cargo, admiral, member ships and cargo. For systems: happiness and its trend, location, starport rating (marked if it was just built or upgraded), status (Normal, UA for under attack, or INT for interdicted), population, infrastructure, habitability, MR/AR/2R, AU and IU stockpiles, and the tech artifacts, leaders, trade routes and Combat Troops on the system. Ships and systems sharing a square with something you do not own are marked, since they bear closer scrutiny.
   - **Scanned ships, fleets and systems** of other races (the Scans section of the report, and the map). These are laid out as above, except that the owner is displayed; long range scans don't show posture or combat role; long range scans of systems only show owner, name and location; and a scanned ship shows the position it moved from rather than where it is going. Systems that are not owned by any empire show up as "Unowned" if they have no population, or "Rebels" if they are populated systems that rebelled (see "POPULATION"). Rebel-held systems just sit and wait for someone to take them over.
8. **The map** of the universe as you know it, built from your scans (see "THE MAP AND ITS LAYERS"). Systems that you have seen are always displayed, along with the latest name and owner you know of. Systems you know about due to a CD-ROM of Ancient Wisdom (a random event that reveals the location of a few systems you have never seen) are displayed as unowned, unnamed systems. The map also remembers the habitability and the metal rich / agriculture rich flags of every system you have had a short range scan of, as of that scan (habitability can change, so the map says which turn it is from), and it shades the squares you have ever scanned differently from those you are scanning now and those you have never scanned, so you can see where you could have missed a system.

All coordinates you see are relative to your own empire: your home system is always at the centre of the board in your own view, 50,50 on the 100 board, 62,62 on the 125 board, 37,37 on the 75 board and 25,25 on the 50 board. Other players see the galaxy from their own home systems, so trading coordinates with another race takes a little arithmetic (and a little trust).

## STRATEGY AND TACTICS TIPS FOR NEW PLAYERS

The following information is based on previous games; it may not be the best strategy for you.

Games in the past have typically broken into three phases. The first phase is the expansion phase, where you try to grab as many systems as you can. The second phase is economic development; purchasing and installing tech, trying to maximize population growth, etc. to improve your empire's NNP standing. The final phase is war.

In the first phase, concentrate on expanding as quickly as possible. Because of the relative coordinate system, it is very difficult for people to determine how big an empire you own. Meet them as far out as you can, and set boundaries at the outermost limit you can get agreement for. Load transports with 2 population points and 20 IUs. Drop off 1 population point and 10 IUs per system, using the 10 IUs to buy an infrastructure. Because uncrowded systems tend to be very happy, the population will quickly expand past the two or three you could have dropped off anyway, and it allows you to send the transport on to another system immediately. This strategy is called the "virus" approach. Avoid building warships and CTs unless unavoidable. The only exception is very valuable systems, such as your home system (remember, Fast Frigates are an early technology and allow a ship to be within one move of a system but outside the system's scanning range). If it is unavoidable, because of hostilities by an adjoining race, take comfort in the fact that the attacker tends to be as screwed as the defender in these cases, because expending resources on war keeps you off the exponential growth curve in this phase. Get in touch with everyone you see. Sharing information is critical in determining who is winning in these stages, before spies become available, and knowing who is winning is significant in positioning yourself for later phases of the game. Additionally, technology sharing cartels (using the "Gift Tech" order) are extremely advantageous in reducing the costs of purchasing technology. The more races participating, the cheaper the cost of technology for each player. Finally, keep happiness in the VH to EH range to keep population growth high and to collect any extra "I love the emperor day" bonuses.

In the second phase, usually starting around turn 15 plus or minus a few, taking out NPR systems and an occasional raid on a nearby, poorly defended system may be advantageous, and you should defend your high value worlds against sneak attacks by Fast Frigates. However, primary emphasis should be on improving productivity of your systems. Again, keep your happiness high to keep the population growing. This also keeps you positioned for emergency measures if you suddenly need to shift production to IU or EUs, since happiness will take a couple of turns to drop to NH or UH (of course, once it does, it takes an equally long time to crawl back up to VH or EH). This is also the time to cement alliances. Because it is easier to defend than attack in this game, it is rare when one player is on the warpath by themselves. Alliances win or lose the game in the final analysis.

In the third phase, usually starting around turn twenty five or so, the easy targets (empires that have not done well) tend to become targets of opportunity for small coalitions of stronger groups. This is usually the turn other races' Cruisers or Light Cruisers start to show up on your scans. Because defensive ships are so much cheaper than attack ships (both in terms of the technology cost and actual ship cost per attack point fielded), surprise is important to success. This is also when larger alliances are beginning to coalesce. Around turn 40-50, there are usually two or three large groups and a few independents fighting it out for supremacy.

In the second and third phases, population is high enough that population growth is very hard to keep up. Use the morale boost from capturing systems to land population on your best worlds from transports (once Extended Use of System is installed so you can get them past 10 billion) in order to keep them expanding at a reasonable pace. Remember that you get the morale boost from capturing a system the next turn, and supplement it with a high AU profile the same turn.

A few tactics hints. Shift Transports with cargo (particularly Combat Troops) to "RAID" combat role. This protects them from space attacks. Watch out for MCS and Armed Transports, however... they will actually fire on the system, potentially destroying the real estate you are trying to capture! Shift these to GROUND COMBAT instead. Transports start in "GROUND COMBAT", so your Space Combat and Multi-Role ships are fired on before them. Shift scouts and empty transports to "SPACE COMBAT" and warships to "MULTI ROLE" if you are not concerned about losing the non-combatants, or are outnumbered. Scouts and transports will absorb enemy fire first, allowing warships to survive and attack a second round.

If you are going to back-stab an empire you are allied with, use the EWA order, not a declare war (DW); this shifts you to neutral while leaving your opponent in allied status, allowing you to get initiative in subsequent conflicts (whereas a DW order will place you and your opponent in "at war" status).

Watch out for where your SISD stockpile is being replenished from. While you can have resources automatically taken from different systems if your supply depot is not large enough to pay for ship maintenance by setting the AUTOSISD flag, it is much better to plan your stockpile replenishment. Remember that any single system can become a point of vulnerability to spies; with sufficient numbers of spies a system can be forced into rebellion, eliminating any SISD order for the turn. Also, ships can end up greater than four moves from a system you own and cost a bit more than expected. As a result, it is wise to keep some contingency funds in your Supply Depot.

If you have space superiority, RAID may be a better option than ground attack. With some luck, you will eliminate Planetary Defenses (or better), reducing ground force effectiveness against space attacks.

Also recognize that any system with greater than 10 billion people due to Extended Use of System tech is likely to face a sudden loss of excess population if EUS is wiped out by a raid.

## ORDERS

### ORDER FORMATS

Every order can be given from the forms in the game screen (see "GIVING ORDERS"), and every order can also be typed in the classic format in the Orders tab's classic order text (see "THE CLASSIC ORDER TEXT"): one order per line, the order code followed by its values, separated by white space (blanks or tabs). Orders are case sensitive (`MO`, not `mo`; and a leader named "Crosko" is not "CROSKO"). A `#` on its own starts a comment: a line beginning with `#` is ignored, and anything after a `#` following the order is ignored too. (`#U 32 A 1`, with no white space after the `#`, is not a comment; it is "unknown order #U".) If you put extra words after an order without a `#`, you will get a warning, in case you meant one of the optional values.

Each entry below gives the action, then the classic code and syntax; the forms show the same code and fill in the values for you. Your orders form a queue (the draft in the Orders tab) that runs top to bottom, and you can reorder the queue before you submit.

The **Check orders** button runs your queued orders against the current state of the game and tells you which would fail and why (not enough EUs, you don't own that system, and so on). It is a validation, not a turn preview: it doesn't move anything, roll any dice, or show you what other players will do.

The examples in this chapter are written in the classic format, and the results are shown the way the old printed turn results showed them (`*** ERROR ...`). On screen, Check orders and the order results mark each order ✓, or ✗ with the reason.

### SEQUENCING TRICKS

Since orders occur "instantaneously" in the order you specify them (the order queue runs top to bottom), you need to watch for a few things. For one, when buying things, put the least important last:

```
PT 6        #  GEE!  I can really use this technology in a few turns!
BS Earth D  #  Oh, yes, the Destroyer to keep my homesystem
            #     from being overrun by the ravaging Bug people
        *** ERROR - insufficient EUs in stockpile to build this ship
```

When capturing an uninhabited system, always put system profile changes, AU profile changes, AU or IU unloads and builds after unloading pop from the ship:

```
U 32 P 2
        *** You've captured unowned system Hoggle
U 32 I 20
CSP Hoggle 25 25 50
BI Hoggle 2
```

works, while

```
CSP Hoggle 25 25 50
                ***ERROR:  You don't own that system
BI Hoggle 2
                ***ERROR:  You don't own that system
U 32 I 20   # giving it to the system, but I'm about to own it anyway
U 32 P 2
        *** You've captured unowned system Hoggle
```

doesn't.

Likewise, if you want to buy an artifact the same turn you buy the prerequisite technology:

```
PT 2
        *** You have purchased Extended Home Computing
BT Hoggle EHC
```

works,

```
BT Hoggle EHC
                  ***ERROR:  You don't have the necessary tech
PT 2
        *** You have purchased Extended Home Computing
```

doesn't.

Finally, remember that ship movement orders set DESIRED location; the ship doesn't actually move until the "movement" phase. Thus the following set of orders for ship 2 over system "Warpoint":

```
L 2 P 1
MS 2 Humbug    # MOVE TO SYSTEM ONLY 2 SQUARES AWAY
U 2 P 1
```

will load ship 2 with 1 bi of pop from Warpoint, set desired location to system Humbug, then unload the pop back to Warpoint. During the movement phase, the empty ship will move to Humbug.

### SYSTEM ORDERS

Select one of your systems on the map: the selection panel has buttons for the common system orders and an "Other orders" picker for the rest. The Production tab writes system profile orders (CSP) for you, the Empire tab's systems table takes you to any of your systems, and the Production tab's ★ flag offers "Install…" (BT) for the technologies a system can install.

#### Rename a system
Classic code: `NSY [old system name] [new system name]`

The new system name may only be 12 characters long, and must not be the name of another system. A few comments; generally, change system name at the END OF YOUR ORDERS. This protects you from having the order fail for some reason (like it is a duplicate name of some other system) and having all subsequent orders for that system fail (since they will be using the new name, which will not have been applied; in fact, for a duplicate name, you will get lots of "you don't own that system" errors). Second, use this order sparingly. Changing system names every turn will indeed screw over players using the MS order to send ships to your systems and not looking very carefully at their map, but this is not the intent of the order.

#### Change system profile
Classic code: `CSP [system name] [%AU 10-100] [%IU 10-100] [%EU 10-100]`

Sets the system profile [ %AU / %IU / %EU ] (note that the total of the three values must add up to 100 or the order will be rejected).

Example to make system Humbug produce 25% AUs, 50% IUs, and 25% EUs:

```
CSP Humbug 25 50 25
```

#### Change AU profile (percentage)
Classic code: `CAP [system name] [percentage 0-100]`

Example to make system Humbug move 33% of its AU stockpile to the population each turn:

```
CAP Humbug 33
```

#### Change AU profile (number of AUs)
Classic code: `CAPN [system name] [integer value 0-100]`

This order releases a specific number of AUs to your population each turn instead of a percentage of the stockpile.

Example to make system Humbug provide 10 AUs to the population each turn from its AU stockpile:

```
CAPN Humbug 10
```

#### Shift IUs to supply depot
Classic code: `SISD [system name] [number of IUs, 0 or more]`

Shift IUs into your supply depot to maintain ships. (Note: This order has some unusual properties. It sets the DESIRED number of IUs for the system to shift to the Supply Depot; a later SISD for the same system replaces the earlier one rather than adding to it. IUs are actually shifted in the maintenance phase. Since this phase comes after production, it means you may use the system production from the turn to maintain your ships. If you attempt to shift more IUs than the system has in its stockpile after production, all IUs in the stockpile will be shifted. Note also that random events occur after maintenance, so a random event cannot wipe out your IU stockpile and therefore leave you with insufficient IUs to maintain your fleet. There is one situation, however, where you can have a problem... if spies lower morale enough on the system to cause it to rebel, or the system is captured before maintenance.)

Example to set DESIRED number of IUs for system Humbug to place in the Supply Depot to 20:

```
SISD Humbug 20
```

#### Counter intelligence
Classic code: `CI [system name] [amount of EUs / 10]`

Hire those bodyguards and staff up the FORCE:GROUND marine brigade guarding your treasured technology. Increases your chance of catching a spy by [base chance] \* (#EUs + 10) / 10 for all spy orders. It also decreases the chance of success for spy activities by [base chance] / ((#EUs+10)/10). Counter intelligence lasts for the turn it is bought. See "Biomodification Technology" for more on spies.

Example to catch spies at four times the base rate (almost automatically catching everything but the lowest probability activities), and quarter the chances for a successful order at a cost of 30 EUs on system Bawana:

```
CI Bawana 3
```

#### Build a combat unit
Classic code: `BCU [system name] [M / S / etc.] [optional posture A, D or E] [optional count 1-50]`

The posture defaults to A; E is for infantry only (see Combat Troop orders for the posture explanation, and the Technology list and "CHARTS" for other unit types).

The count builds several troops with one order: `BCU Gander S 3` is the same as giving `BCU Gander S` three times (each troop uses half a starport build, its IUs and its EUs, at double cost while the system is under attack). If one can't be built (no starport capacity, IUs or EUs left, or the militia limit), the order stops there: the troops already built stay, and the result says, for example, "Built 2 of 3 Shock troops at Gander: ... The other 1 was not built: ..." with the reason. If not even the first can be built, the order fails.

#### Build a ship
Classic code: `BS [system name] [S / T / F / etc.] [optional posture F, N or H] [optional combat role SC, MR, GC or RD] [optional count 1-50]`

Defaults to N SC, except that transports (Transport and MassTransit) default to N GC: in Ground Combat a transport is fired on after your Space Combat and Multi-Role ships, and since it has no attack it never shoots at the system (see the Technology list and "CHARTS" for other ship types, and ship orders for the posture and combat role explanations). Note that you cannot do "G" because that would allow the ship to move the same turn it was built.

In the browser the ship type picker lists the classes by kind, as the rules group them: **Scouts** (Scout, SuperScout, Megascout: the scout class), **Transports** (Transport, MassTransit), then the four ship lines of the Mixed Fleet Bonus, **Standard line**, **Offensive line**, **Defensive line** and **Fast line** (the armed cargo ships, ArmedTrans and Multirole_CS, are warships and sit in their line). Within each group they run from the cheapest technology to the dearest, then by cost. Each group's heading says how many of its classes you can build now; a class you can't build yet stays in its place, greyed, with "needs <technology>".

The count builds several ships with one order: `BS Gander T 3` builds three Transports, exactly as three `BS Gander T` orders would (each uses one starport build, its IUs and its EUs). They all get the posture and role given. If one can't be built the order stops there, keeps what it built and says why: "Built 2 of 3 Transports at Gander: 1234, 5678. The other 1 was not built: The class 2 starport at Gander has no capacity left this turn: ...". Check orders shows the same, as a warning.

#### Build infrastructure
Classic code: `BI [system name] [amount of infrastructure to buy]`

Costs 10 IUs per point.

#### Destroy infrastructure
Classic code: `DI [system name] [amount of infrastructure to destroy]`

Costs 5 EUs per point. The system must be in normal status.

#### Build a tech artifact
Classic code: `BT [system name] [tech type]`

You must own the technology (see the Tech list for options). Building a spy is `BT [system name] S`.

#### Destroy a tech artifact
Classic code: `DT [system name] [tech type]`

The system must be yours and in normal status.

#### Build or upgrade a starport by one class
Classic code: `BSP [system name]`

#### Reduce a starport by one class
Classic code: `DSP [system name]`

Reduces starport size by one, at a cost of 10 EUs.

Examples:

```
BCU Gander S        #  Build myself a SHOCK TROOP!
BS Gander F         #  Build myself a FRIGATE!
BS Gander T 3       #  ... and three Transports
BI Gander 3         #  Build 3 additional IF
BT Gander EHC       #  Build Extended Home Comp.
BSP Gander          #  Add one class to Gander Starport
DSP Gander          #  Reduce Gander Starport by one
```

#### System loads cargo onto a ship
Classic code: `SL [ship number] [A / I / P] [number to load]`

Puts things on transports in the system. Note that AUs and IUs count as a twentieth of a cargo point, so any number of AU and IUs between 1 and 20 will take one cargo point. This is very similar to the "L" or Load command. The only difference is that in this case the system gives things to the ship rather than the other way around, and as a result can be used to load items on a ship that the player who owns the system does not own.

Example to load two pop, 11 AU and 9 IU on a transport no. 19:

```
SL 19 A 11
SL 19 I 9
SL 19 P 2
```

#### Teleport cargo by Farcaster
Classic code: `TP [from system] [A / I / C] [number of AUs or IUs, or CT number] [to system]`

See Farcaster Portal in "TECHNOLOGIES".

#### Send system information
Classic code: `SSI [race name] [your system name]`

Sends the race a full (short range) scan of one of your systems, as if they had scanned it themselves. Handy for proving to an ally that the system really is as well defended as you say. You must have contact with the race, and you can't send it to a race you are at war with.

#### Release a system
Classic code: `SR [system name]`

Release a system with 1 pop or less. The system must be in normal status. The population is abandoned and the system returns to unowned space. Like a system that dies out, it loses its AU and IU stockpiles, infrastructure, starport, artifacts and trade routes, so whoever settles it next starts from bare rock.

### SHIP ORDERS

Select the ship on the map (or click its row in the Forces tab, Ships): the selection panel has buttons for moving, intercepting, guarding, posture, role, cargo and troops, and an "Other orders" picker for the rest. With the ship selected, right-click a square (or press M) to move it there. Gift ship (GS) is also on the Races tab.

#### Disband ship
Classic code: `DS [ship number]`

Any troops aboard die with the ship.

#### Salvage ship
Classic code: `SS [ship number]`

Recover 75% of the original cost of a ship at a starport on a system in "NORMAL" status. Costs one "build" for the turn (i.e., if a class one starport salvages a ship it cannot build another that turn). Any cargo and troops aboard are put ashore.

#### Change ship posture
Classic code: `SP [ship number] [H / N / F]`

Hostile, Neutral or Friendly. See "SHIPS" for how these postures affect encounters between ships.

Example to make ship 93 take a hostile posture:

```
SP 93 H
```

#### Change ship combat role
Classic code: `CSCR [ship number] [SC, MR, GC, or RD]`

See "COMBAT" for how these options affect a ship's actions in combat.

Example to make ship 93 take a Raid Combat Role:

```
CSCR 93 RD
```

#### Move ship to X,Y
Classic code: `MO [ship number] [x] [y]`

(Note: ship will always take shortest path, even across wrap around borders.) (Note: remember, this sets a ship's DESIRED location, the ship actually moves during the "movement phase".)

Example to make ship 93 move to 54 89:

```
MO 93 54 89
```

#### Move ship to a system
Classic code: `MS [ship number] [system name]`

Note that this is just an easier way to specify a ship's destination, and it only works for systems you have seen. (Note: ship will always take shortest path, even across wrap around borders.) (Note: remember, this sets a ship's DESIRED location, the ship actually moves during the "movement phase".)

Example to make ship 93 move to system Guacamole:

```
MS 93 Guacamole
```

#### Intercept a ship
Classic code: `I [ship number] [ship number to intercept]`

For intercepting enemy ships, or joining up with own ships. If you intercept someone who is intercepting someone else, things will usually work out sensibly: the non-interception moves occur, then the interceptions of moved ships are done and done again, until all interceptions have been done. Circles of ships intercepting each other close toward a common center point. (Note: ship will always take shortest path, even across wrap around borders.)

Example to make ship 93 move to ship 123:

```
I 93 123
```

#### Guard
Classic code: `G [ship number]`

The ship will stand unless an enemy ship is within one move; if so, it will move to that ship (closest first, randomly for ships at the same distance). It will not move to ships owned by allied players.

Example to make ship 93 watch for incoming ships:

```
G 93
```

#### Load, unload, or unload as a gift
Classic codes: `L [ship number] [A / I / P] [number to load]`, `U [ship number] [A / I / P] [number to unload]`, `UG [ship number] [A / I / P] [number to unload]`

Load / Unload cargo (IU, AUs, population). Note that you can do as many of these as you want, as long as you do not exceed the capacity of the transporting vehicle. So you can Unload 3 pop, load 16 AUs, and load 14 IUs all in one turn. Unload (U) and Unload Gift (UG) are the same except regular Unloads will fail for a system you do not own (this is usually only significant if you are in a race for an unowned system; it would be nasty if the other race landed first and then you "gave" them your population).

Example to make ship 93 load 2 billion pop, 10 AUs and 10 IUs:

```
L 93 P 2
L 93 A 10
```

#### Merge fleets
Classic code: `MGF [fleet name] [fleet to merge into]`

A web-game order (not in the classic game). #### Trade technology with an NPR
Classic code: `TTNPR [tech you give, or ANY cost] [tech you want, or ANY cost] [NPR name]`

This is a tech-for-tech swap between you and an NPR, equivalent to each of you doing a "Gift Tech" to one another: each side pays half the cost of the technology it gives. The NPR can accept or refuse the offer (the cheaper your offer is compared to what you want, the less likely it accepts; an even swap is always accepted), and all the conditions for the Gift Tech order still apply. A refused offer still counts as your attempt for the turn; an offer that fails its checks (they already have the tech, you lack the money, and so on) doesn't, so you can drop it and try another. Remember that only one tech a turn comes by purchase (PT), gift (GT) or trade (TTNPR): if you also buy a tech with PT, buy it by crash research (CRP) instead to leave room for the trade. If the NPR offered you exactly this swap in the chat (see THE OTHER TABS, Chat), it always accepts it on the turn the offer names, whatever the costs; everything else about the order still applies (you must own your technology and be able to use theirs, have the EUs, not be at war, and make only one TTNPR a turn). Check orders tells you "they will accept".

**ANY.** Either side, or both, can be `ANY` and a cost instead of a technology number: "any technology costing exactly that much". The cost is a technology's list price (100, 250, 500, 1000, 2000, 3000 or 4000 EU; see TECHNOLOGIES), and the order form offers just those. You can write it as two words, `ANY 1000`, or one, `ANY:1000`. When the turn runs the NPR picks:

- for what you want (`ANY 1000` second): one of its own 1000 EU technologies that you don't have and can use (you own its prerequisites), the one it values least;
- for what you give (`ANY 1000` first): one of your 1000 EU technologies that it doesn't have and can use, the one it values most.

Everything else is as for a named technology: the acceptance chance comes from the two costs (equal costs are always accepted), each side pays half the cost of what it gives, and the one-tech-a-turn and one-attempt-a-turn rules apply. If there is nothing to pick ("The Groachi have no 1000 EU tech you lack and could use", or "You have no 1000 EU tech the Groachi lack and could use"), the trade fails without using your attempt for the turn. Check orders can't know the NPR's technologies, so for an ANY order it checks only what you can know (contact, war, your money and technologies) and says the NPR will pick when the turn runs. The turn results name what you gave and got: "The Groachi accept: you gave Core Mining (1000) and received Systems Integration (1000)". An NPR that doesn't know your technologies may offer you one of its own "for any 1000 EU technology of yours that we lack"; its Accept button adds `TTNPR ANY 1000 [their tech] [NPR name]`, and it keeps its word if what it picks is one it said it would take.

Example to offer Extended Home Computing for Powered Armor to NPR race Homeboys:

```
TTNPR 2 1 Homeboys
```

Example to offer the Groachi any one of your 1000 EU technologies they lack for any one of theirs that you lack:

```
TTNPR ANY 1000 ANY 1000 Groachi
```

#### Purchase technology
Classic code: `PT [technology number]`

You may only purchase or receive (as a gift) one tech per turn.

Example to purchase Extended Home Computing:

```
PT 2
```

#### Crash research program
Classic code: `CRP [technology number]`

Buys a technology at twice its normal cost, on top of your one PT for the turn. You may run more than one CRP in a turn, each at twice cost; you still need the prerequisites, and you still can't get more than one Future Technology a turn.

#### List technology
Classic code: `LT`

Lists all tech owned by your empire.

#### Automatically resupply the IU supply depot
Classic codes: `AUTOSISD` / `NOAUTOSISD`

IU stockpiles on randomly selected systems are used to place the necessary supplies to keep all ships alive in your stockpile (only the shortfall is moved). Systems must be in "NORMAL" status. AUTOSISD is on by default; NOAUTOSISD turns it off.

#### Autogenerate orders on missed turn
Classic code: `AOMT ON` / `AOMT OFF`

A race setting, on unless you turn it off (it is also the AOMT checkbox on the Empire tab). With it on, a turn the host forces before you have submitted still runs for you: your unsubmitted draft runs as if submitted, or, if the draft is empty, the computer writes that turn's orders from your own view, with nothing hidden from you, and leaves your diplomacy and messages alone (see "WHEN THE TURN RUNS" in "PLAYING IN YOUR BROWSER"). With it off, a forced turn runs with no orders from you and your draft is ignored. The setting takes effect as soon as the order is saved in your draft, so it already covers the turn you are writing, and it stays until you change it; the order then runs with your turn and confirms the setting. Nobody else learns of it.

```
AOMT ON
```

#### Change race name
Classic code: `CRN [new name]`

You may only do this once. Race names must be 15 characters or less, with no white space (BLOW HARDS is wrong, BLOW_HARDS is right), and must not be another race's name. Names are case sensitive. The race name will be echoed back to you.

#### Resign
Classic code: `RESIGN`

You leave the game. Your empire becomes an NPR, and is buffed up so that your neighbors don't get an easy meal.

### MISCELLANEOUS ORDERS

These are in the Orders tab's order picker; Message is also a button on each race in the Races tab.

#### Send a message
Classic code: `MESSAGE [optional race names]`

Sends messages to other races; they are delivered with the turn results. (For conversation between turns, there is also the Chat tab.) In the form you tick the recipients (none means every race) and type the text; the Races tab's Message button starts one to that race. The typed format, for the classic order text, is slightly unusual:

```
MESSAGE [optional racenames]
optional further MESSAGE lines
as many lines of message text as you care to type
.
```

The optional racenames may contain as many races as you want to receive the message. If you specify no races, the message is broadcast to all other races. If you find the number of races do not fit on one line conveniently, you may specify further races by using additional MESSAGE lines in the same format. On subsequent lines, you may enter whatever text you wish to send to the different races. The message is terminated with a period as the first character of a line. The MESSAGE and "." keyword may not be used as the first word of a message text line. Be very careful to terminate your message properly, as incorrect termination will result in all subsequent orders being interpreted as part of the message! You may want to put messages at the end of your orders to be safe.

Example to send the message "Roses are Red, Violets are Blue." to all races:

```
MESSAGE
Roses are Red,
Violets are Blue.
.
```

Example to send the same message to just six specific races:

```
MESSAGE Blutos Mangos Gollywogs Bratwursts
MESSAGE Googly_eyes Ratfinks
Roses are Red,
Violets are Blue.
.
```

Example of a bad message (with embedded MESSAGE and ".", as well as no white space after the actual message terminator):

```
MESSAGE Googly_eyes
Are you getting the
MESSAGE I am sending you?
. I don't think so.
.end of my message
```

#### Echo
Classic code: `ECHO [anything]`

Echo will simply show the line in your order results. In the email game it was used to mark versions of your orders, so you could tell which version was processed; in the browser the orders that run are always the ones you last submitted, so it is just a note to yourself.

Example to show the line 'Version 7 of FirBolg turn 14 orders':

```
ECHO Version 7 of FirBolg turn 14 orders
```
